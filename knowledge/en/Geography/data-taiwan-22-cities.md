# Taiwan's 22 Counties and Cities in Data: A 151-fold Gap Between Crowded and Empty, a Generation Apart Between Old and Young

Driving from Taipei's Xinyi District, heading south and east, you eventually arrive in Taitung City. The navigation shows over 300 kilometers; it takes less than a day to drive. But if you change your focus to another metric—how many people live in every square kilometer outside your window—that journey feels like traveling between two different countries. In Taipei City, where Xinyi District is located, 8,975 people are crowded into every square kilometer. In Taitung County, where Taitung City is located, there are only 59 people per square kilometer. On the same island, with the same passport, the density difference is a staggering 151-fold.

This number is not an outlier trick; it represents a real disparity within Taiwan. We are accustomed to discussing "Taiwan" as a single entity—its economy, its elections, its low birth rate—but when you spread out the full set of county and city data from the Ministry of the Interior's Department of Household Registration for the end of 2025, you find there is no such thing as a homogeneous Taiwan. Some are queuing for red lights in a concrete jungle; others drive ten minutes without seeing a second car. Some counties are still growing, while most are shrinking. The youngest corners and the oldest corners are separated by nearly an entire generation.


This article aims to do one thing: use official data to draw a scaled, verifiable portrait of the entire island. Once drawn, you will see a face that is deeply fragmented and rapidly aging.[^1]

> **30-Second Overview:** At the end of 2025, Taiwan's total population was 23,299,132 (approximately 23.3 million), having decreased for 23 consecutive months, with newborns falling below 110,000 for the first time. In the same year, Taiwan officially entered a "super-aged society," where one in every five people is over 65. However, the national average masks massive internal disparities: the gap between the highest and lowest population density is 151-fold, the scale of population differs by 297-fold, and the degree of aging differs by nearly a generation. People are concentrating in the six special municipalities; the frontier of aging is not in the cities but in the east, the outlying islands, and agricultural counties. The youngest area is Hsinchu County, supported by the Hsinchu Science Park. This is a Taiwan that is both fragmented and aging together.[^2]

## 70% of the People, 30% of the Land

First, consider a fact that is easily overlooked: Taiwanese people live in highly concentrated areas.

At the end of 2025, the six special municipalities (Taipei, New Taipei, Taoyuan, Taichung, Tainan, and Kaohsiung) had a combined population of 16,278,931, accounting for 69.87% of Taiwan's total population. In other words, nearly seven out of every ten Taiwanese live in these six cities. Yet, the land area of these six municipalities accounts for only 30.12% of Taiwan's total area. Seventy percent of the people are squeezed into thirty percent of the land. The remaining thirty percent of the people are scattered across the other seventy percent of the land. This is the first structural reality of Taiwan's population distribution.

```tw-stat
23.3 million | Total population of Taiwan | Decreasing for 23 consecutive months as of end-2025
20.06% | Proportion of population aged 65+ | Officially crossed the super-aged threshold in 2025
69.87% | Proportion of population in six special municipalities | Yet they live on only 30% of the land
Source: Population statistics from the Department of Household Registration, Ministry of the Interior (Total population, 65+ proportion, and six-municipality proportion as of end-2025) and data on municipal restructuring[^1][^4]
```

This 7-to-3 pattern was not naturally formed; it has a clear institutional starting point. On December 25, 2010, Taiwan overhauled its administrative map: Taipei County was upgraded to New Taipei City, Taichung County/City merged, Tainan County/City merged, and Kaohsiung County/City merged. Along with the original Taipei City, five special municipalities were born simultaneously. Four years later, on December 25, 2014, Taoyuan was upgraded to the sixth municipality. In just four years, Taiwan went from two special municipalities to six, and the scales of resources, budgets, and infrastructure tilted accordingly.[^4]

Upgrading is never just about changing a name. The general allocation grants, personnel quotas, and autonomous revenue sources received by special municipalities are far higher than those of regular counties. Where construction goes, job opportunities grow, and young people move. The system drew a line first, and the population flowed along it. The concentration in the six municipalities we see today is, to some extent, the fruit of that 2010 restructuring over a decade later.

The following chart breaks down "where Taiwanese people live" more clearly. New Taipei City alone holds 17.4% of the national population; Taichung 12.3%, Kaohsiung 11.7%, Taipei 10.5%, Taoyuan 10.1%, and Tainan 7.9%—the six municipalities combined account for nearly seventy percent. Meanwhile, the other 16 counties and cities scattered across Taiwan, from the north to the outlying islands, combine for only 30.1%.


If you stare at this waffle chart for a moment, a question arises: what kind of life do those 30% scattered across 70% of the land lead? The answer lies in density.

## From Most Crowded to Most Empty: A 151-fold Difference

Population concentration speaks of "how many people," but density speaks of "how crowded." The latter better conveys the distance between the two Taiwans.

Taipei City, with 8,975 people per km², is the most crowded place in Taiwan. Crowding is a daily reality in Taipei: the MRT is packed during peak hours, you wait through multiple red lights, and buildings are tall and narrow. Following closely is Hsinchu City at 4,376 people/km², which is less than half of Taipei's density. Then comes Keelung City at 2,710 people/km². Among just these three most crowded cities, the density already drops precipitously.

```tw-bars
Taipei City | 8,975 people/km²
Hsinchu City | 4,376 people/km²
Keelung City | 2,710 people/km²
Changhua County | 1,126 people/km²
Hualien County | 68 people/km²
Taitung County | 59 people/km² | Least crowded in Taiwan
Source: Department of Household Registration, end of 2025
```

Moving your gaze to the other end of this bar chart, the disparity truly becomes visible. Changhua County, with 1,126 people per km², is relatively dense for an agricultural county. By the time you reach [Hualien County](/en/geography/hualien-county/), the number drops to 68. At the very bottom, Taitung County has only 59 people per square kilometer. The number of people living in one square kilometer of Taipei is equivalent to what it takes to fill 151 square kilometers of Taitung. This is the true face of a 151-fold difference—driving from Xinyi District all the way to Taitung and watching the population thin out through your window.

There is also a geographical reality supporting this. Hualien County is the largest county in Taiwan, covering 4,628 km², spanning almost the entire eastern side of the Central Mountain Range. Large area, few people, and many mountains mean that the densities of Hualien and Taitung are naturally diluted to the lowest in Taiwan. Meanwhile, the smallest, [Lienchiang County](/en/geography/lienchiang-county/) (Matsu), is only 28.8 km². Density is half human choice and half determined by innate topography.

Common wisdom often equates "high population density" with progress and "low density" with backwardness, but this relationship actually reverses cause and effect. Taitung's low density is not because it failed to develop, but because it sits against great mountains facing the Pacific Ocean—it was born with a geography of vast land and sparse people. Hualien's "emptiness" contains Taroko Gorge, the Coastal Range, and Taiwan's most complete mountain landscapes. Density numbers only tell you how crowded it is; they do not tell you the value of that land. When you arrange all 22 counties and cities on a spectrum from crowded to empty, you see an island where different terrains have fostered different ways of life.

## Giants and Dust Motes

If density compares "crowdedness," population scale compares "size." The disparity in this dimension is even more extreme than density.

The most populous county in Taiwan is New Taipei City, with 4,044,831 people—a single city comparable to many nations. The least populous is Lienchiang County (Matsu), with only 13,621 people. The population of New Taipei is 297 times that of Lienchiang. Even if you moved the entire population of Lienchiang into New Taipei, they wouldn't even fill a tiny fraction of it. Holding the same Republic of China passport, one county's "entirety" is merely a dust mote in the eyes of another.

This scale disparity translates directly into governance challenges. New Taipei must manage transportation, housing, long-term care, and waste for four million people; Lienchiang must figure out how to prevent its 13,000 islanders from continuing to leave, how to maintain a single hospital, and how to keep the last ferry running. The same laws and central policies land very differently when applied to a 297-fold population gap. When we speak of "local government," our minds often default to an urban scale, but many counties and cities in Taiwan operate an entire governmental machine on the scale of a dust mote.

However, one must avoid an intuitive trap: small scale does not mean young people. You might assume that outlying islands and small counties are both small and old because young people leave, leaving only the elderly; conversely, you might think large cities are both big and young due to abundant resources and opportunities. Real data refutes both assumptions. While Lienchiang has the smallest population, its aging rate is 17.14%, which is actually lower than several large counties. Meanwhile, Taipei City—the fourth most populous and incredibly prosperous—has the highest aging rate in Taiwan at 24.18%. There is no clean correlation between scale and aging. To understand how Taiwan is aging, you need a different map.

## The Frontier of Aging is Not in the Cities

In 2025, Taiwan crossed a threshold: the proportion of the population aged 65 and over reached 20.06%, officially becoming a "super-aged society" as defined by the World Health Organization. This means 4.67 million people in Taiwan are over 65; one in every five Taiwanese is an elder. Furthermore, among the 22 counties and cities, 14 have already seen their aging rates surpass 20%. Aging has become a common backdrop for the entire island.

But this national average of 20.06% flattens the intense internal differences. The following table lists the aging rate and aging index for all 22 counties and cities; this is the part of the article where you should pause and look closely. To explain how to read the "Aging Index": it is calculated by dividing the population aged 65+ by the population aged 0–14, then multiplying by 100. A value of 100 means there are as many elderly as children; a higher number indicates a "top-heavy" area, with more elderly people and fewer children.

When reading this table, pay special attention to the Aging Index column. The highest in Taiwan is Chiayi County at 291.69, meaning that in Chiayi, every child corresponds to nearly three elderly people. This is followed by Kinmen County (255.57) and Keelung City (240.21). These three places share a common characteristic: they are all agricultural counties, outlying islands, or old port cities where industries have moved away; none of them make the list of Taiwan's most prosperous cities. The deepest frontier of aging falls on these corners that have long faced the outflow of young people. When the youth leave for work or education, those who remain grow older, and newborns cannot replenish the numbers, causing the aging index to soar.

So, where is the youngest corner of Taiwan? The answer will surprise many: it is not in some utopia, but next to high-tech factories. Hsinchu County's aging rate is only 15.08%, the lowest in Taiwan; its aging index is 101.88, a nearly balanced state of one elder to one child, which stands out amidst the island's widespread aging. Hsinchu City (16.16%) and Taoyuan City (16.72%) follow closely. The youth in these three places can be attributed almost entirely to one reason: the Hsinchu Science Park. The semiconductor and technology industries attract a large number of engineers and technicians of childbearing age; they settle down and have children here, forcibly pulling the population structure of these counties toward the younger end. Beyond Taiwan's export figures, the Hsinchu Science Park also supports Taiwan's youngest demographic landscape.

Placing the youngest and oldest ends together makes the disparity even more striking.

```tw-versus
Hsinchu County (Youngest) | Chiayi County (Oldest)
65+ Proportion 15.08% | 65+ Proportion 24.11%
Aging Index 101.88 | Aging Index 291.69
Population still growing | Population continuously declining
Youth supported by Science Park | Aging frontier of agricultural county
Source: Department of Household Registration, end of 2025
```

Hsinchu County's 15.08% versus Chiayi County's 24.11% shows a 9-percentage-point gap in aging rates; the aging index of 101.88 versus 291.69 is nearly a three-fold difference. One is still growing, with people flowing in; the other is continuously losing people, with them flowing out. These two counties on the same island differ by nearly an entire generation in their degree of aging. In a Hsinchu park, you might see young parents pushing strollers; in the countryside of Chiayi, an entire street might be filled with slow-walking elders. Both versions of Taiwan are calculated by the Department of Household Registration's numbers.

This debunks a popular misconception: many believe aging is the exclusive domain of the countryside and outlying islands, and that large cities should be immune to aging due to more young people and opportunities. The data says otherwise. Taipei City's aging rate is 24.18%, the highest in Taiwan; its aging index of 202.06 is also the highest among the six special municipalities. This city, Taiwan's most prosperous and resource-concentrated, is simultaneously the oldest of the six municipalities. The reason is not hard to understand: high housing prices in Taipei push young families toward New Taipei or Taoyuan to start families, leaving those who settled early in the city to gradually age. Urban prosperity cannot stop aging; it just experiences it in a different way. Aging is not a disease affecting certain counties; it is a shared condition for the entire island, differing only in speed and timing.

## One Island, Aging Together

Moving the lens from counties to the whole nation, Taiwan's story of aging has another dimension worth watching: speed.

Taiwan did not just start aging now. In 1993, the proportion of the population aged 65+ exceeded 7%, entering an "aging society" by international definitions. In 2018, this reached 14.05%, entering an "aged society." In 2025, it broke 20%, becoming a "super-aged society." These three milestones look like mere years, but they hide a downhill slope that is accelerating.

```tw-timeline
1993 | Aging Society | 65+ exceeded 7%, Taiwan began to age
2018 | Aged Society | 65+ reached 14%, 25 years since the last milestone
2025 | Super-aged Society | 65+ broke 20%, this stage took only 7 years
Source: Ministry of the Interior statistics and international aging threshold definitions (7% in 1993, 14.05% in 2018, 20% in 2025)[^1]
```

Please look at the intervals on this timeline. It took Taiwan 25 years to go from 7% to 14%; however, it took only 7 years to go from 14% to 20%. This latter stretch took less than one-third of the time of the former. Taiwan is aging, and it is doing so faster and faster. This is rapid even by global standards; while other countries have decades to gradually adjust long-term care, pensions, and medical systems, Taiwan is compressed into a mere seven years, forced to build an entire elderly care system within a very narrow window.

```tw-line
Rise in proportion of population aged 65+ (%)
Year | 65+ Proportion
2000 | 8.6
2010 | 10.7
2020 | 16.1
2025 | 20.06
Source: Ministry of the Interior, National Development Council
```

This rising line makes the acceleration even clearer. In 2000, the proportion of those aged 65+ was 8.6%; in 2010, it was 10.7%—an increase of just over 2 percentage points in ten years. But from 2010 to 2020, the number jumped from 10.7% to 16.1%, and then to 20.06% in 2025. The end of the line is visibly steeper than the beginning. Taiwan's aging curve is bending upward.

The other side of aging is the collapse of births. In 2025, the number of newborns in Taiwan fell below 110,000 for the first time, at only 107,812. Low birth rates and aging are two sides of the same coin: as the elderly population grows, the number of children being added shrinks, causing the entire population structure to become increasingly top-heavy. This is why the aging indices in those counties mentioned earlier are so high: more elderly at the top, too few children at the bottom.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> It is easy to interpret "population decline" as "young people moving from the countryside to cities, so the countryside shrinks and cities grow," as if it were just an internal migration. But the 2025 data reveals something more fundamental: the "natural increase" in every county and city across the nation is negative. In other words, in every single county and city in Taiwan, regardless of whether it is urban or rural, large or small, deaths have exceeded births. This is no longer a question of where people are moving; it is that the entire island is experiencing a demographic contraction. Migration only redistributes a population that is already shrinking; it does not create any new people.

This fact warrants serious reflection. In 2025, only four counties and cities in Taiwan were still growing: Taoyuan, Hsinchu County, Taichung, and Hsinchu City. Furthermore, all of their growth came from "social increase"—meaning the influx of people from other areas—relying on absorbing people from other counties rather than local births. Aside from these four, the populations of the remaining 18 counties and cities all decreased. The largest declines were seen in Kinmen County, Lienchiang County, and Taipei City; note that even the most prosperous Taipei City is on this list of loss. When births in every corner of the nation cannot compensate for deaths, those four growing counties are merely standing temporarily upon the losses of others.

This is why the total population has decreased for 23 consecutive months, falling to 23,299,132. It is a synchronized contraction of the entire island; every county and city is shrinking. The only difference is that some places can temporarily hold their ground through migration, while others have no such buffer.

## A Portrait Drawn by Data

Returning to that initial journey. From Taipei's Xinyi District to Taitung, the people per square kilometer drop from 8,975 to 59, like passing through two different countries. Now you know it is not just a difference in density. Along the way, population scale transforms from giants to dust motes; aging shifts from the youth supported by the Science Park to the aging frontier of agricultural counties. The only constant is that no matter which county or city you stop in, local deaths have already exceeded births.

This is the portrait of Taiwan in 2025: a highly fragmented face that is aging together. Its internal disparities are staggering—a 151-fold difference in density, a 297-fold difference in scale, and an aging gap of nearly a generation; yet its fundamental situation is strikingly consistent: the entire island is contracting, the total population has shrunk for 23 consecutive months, and it has surged from an aged society to a super-aged society within just seven years. The National Development Council estimates that, under medium projections, Taiwan's total population will fall to 14.97 million by 2070, with the proportion of those aged 65+ reaching 46.5%; the demographic dividend will end in 2028. This portrait will not improve on its own.[^3]

Acknowledging Taiwan's internal heterogeneity is necessary to see the island's true challenge: how can a place with such vast internal differences use a single set of policies to simultaneously care for four million people squeezed into a basin and thirteen thousand people guarding outlying islands, while facing both the youngest Hsinchu and the oldest Chiayi? It is fragmented, yet it must act as a whole; it is aging, yet it must find solutions together. Data has drawn this face for us; how we respond to it is the question the island must answer together.
