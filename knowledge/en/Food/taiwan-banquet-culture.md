# Taiwan's _Pān-toh_ Banquets: A Rain-Soaked Works That Still Opens, and a Three-Realm Ritual System Quietly Folding Up

> **30-second overview:** _Pān-toh_ isn't a modern invention — the _Taiwan County Gazetteer_ from the late Kangxi era (Qing) already recorded that Taiwanese "whenever a family has a celebration or the seasons turn, they must feast guests lavishly"[^1]. Neimen District in Kaohsiung, a stretch of mudstone badlands where even crops struggle, once supported roughly 150 teams of head chefs among just 14,000 residents — the densest concentration on the island[^2]. Today the craft is splitting in two: banquet cuisine has entered five-star hotels and the Michelin Guide, even booming overseas[^3]; meanwhile the ritual knowledge that once mobilized entire villages, with head chefs serving as folklore consultants bridging the human, divine, and ghost realms, is quietly vanishing for lack of successors[^4]. What's disappearing isn't the flavor — it's the tacit understanding behind it.

An evening downpour arrived sudden and fierce; within minutes calf-deep water covered the ground.

It was the night veteran Tainan head chef Wang Yi-yong had committed to a 120-table banquet. The canvas frame buckled under the weight of rain, stoves were doused one by one, pots and pans floated on the water's surface, and even the trout waiting to go into the wok swam out of their basins — over a dozen escaped in one go[^5]. Most people would have called it off.

Wang arrived on his rounds, didn't call it off. He had the crew slash the canopy to drain the flood, moved every piece of kitchen gear under the eaves, rolled up his own sleeves, and waded in to catch the fish. That night they lost only a single trout, made up from the reserve-table ingredients. Every guest ate[^5].

For three centuries _pān-toh_ has run on this logic: as long as the host family doesn't say stop, the tables open. That mission-must-succeed discipline has carried every major Taiwanese milestone from birth to funeral, from god-worship to ghost-offering. And now it's moving in two directions at once — one up, one down.

## Restaurants Are the _Bûn-tiûnn_; Roadside Banquets Are the _Bú-tiûnn_

The trade has a common metaphor: chefs who command a fixed restaurant — with air-conditioning and a complete kitchen workflow — work the **_bûn-tiûnn_** (文場, “civil arena”). Those who haul their gear to roadsides, temple plazards, or community centers and erect temporary kitchens work the **_bú-tiûnn_** (武場, “martial arena”) [^5].

The difference between _bûn-tiûnn_ and _bú-tiûnn_ is not culinary skill; it is **uncertainty**. A restaurant’s location, equipment, and staff are the same every day. For a roadside banquet, the venue, the timing, and the crew are all different every time. The same head banquet chef (_tsong-phoo-su_, 總鋪師) might oversee a deity’s birthday banquet (_sîn-bîn_, 神明生) at a Tainan temple plaza this week, and a funeral feast at a mountain clan hall next week. The canvas canopy must be rebuilt, the burners reconnected, the kitchen assistants (_tsuí-kha_, 水腳) reassigned. From a management perspective, this is the highest-difficulty off-site catering model: for a single complete banquet, the _tsong-phoo-su_ is simultaneously executive chef, event director, and logistics coordinator — canvas canopies, tables and chairs, bowls and plates, ingredient suppliers, and the entire labor crew all fall under one person’s command [^5].

That is why cooking technique is merely the baseline; **crisis management is the true core competency**.

> **✦** “What I fear most is guests waiting in vain. The moment I got home I rushed to the phone.” — Neimen _tsong-phoo-su_ **Hsueh Meng-hui** (薛孟輝), recalling the night of Typhoon Morakot [^6]

On the night Typhoon Morakot struck, Hsueh Meng-hui was in Fengshan wielding the ladle (_chhiong-phio_, 掌杓) for a deity’s peace banquet. Wind howled and rain lashed down; the canvas canopy collapsed, an iron frame struck a chef in the head, and the host (_chú-ke_, 主家) finally called off the event [^6]. He sent each kitchen assistant home to Chwei (旗尾) and Shanlin (杉林), then tried to return to Neimen — only to find every bridge washed out. He spent the entire night trapped in a 7-ELEVEN, watching the torrent of the Nanzihsian Creek (楠梓仙溪) surge like an open sea [^6]. What kept him awake all night were the guests who had waited in vain for the banquet to begin: the moment he reached home he rushed to make calls. And the very next day, he still had a Guanyin birthday banquet to execute [^6].

Whether gale-force winds and torrential rain, or bridges severed and roads collapsed — as long as the host does not cancel, the _tsong-phoo-su_ must find a way to put the dishes on the table. “Mission accomplished” has been this trade’s real barrier to entry for three hundred years, long before culinary skill ever was.

## Where Only Thorny Bamboo Survives, a Kingdom of Head Banquet Chefs Grew

![高雄古亭坑層的泥岩惡地，灰白色的山脊一道道裸露、幾乎寸草不生，遠方是稀疏的綠樹與小聚落](/article-images/food/tianliao-moon-world-badlands-2019.webp)

Photo: StevenK234, 2019. [CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tianliao_Moon_World_3.jpg)._

To understand how this banquet-field discipline was forged, one must first look at a piece of land where little grows.

The place with the densest concentration of Taiwan's head banquet chefs (_zǒng pù shī_) is Neimen, Kaohsiung. Neimen sits on the mudrock badlands of the Gutingkeng Formation — the landscape commonly called the "Moon World" — where gray-white slopes lie bare, almost devoid of vegetation, stretching across Neimen, Tianliao, and Yanchao. _Taiwan Panorama_ describes this geology: "Mudrock has low cohesion and permeability; it softens into mire when wet, cracks and flakes when dry, and the high salinity of the marine sedimentary soil makes plant growth difficult."[^7] On these barren badland slopes where even crops struggle, almost only drought-resistant thorny bamboo (_cì zhú_) survives. Only in areas with thicker chalky soil can mangoes and bananas be grown — and because they grow slowly, they are even sweeter.[^7]

The land doesn't sustain people, so people must find another way.

Two coexisting explanations, each with its own sources, account for why Neimen became the hometown of head banquet chefs. One comes from an interview by _The Reporter_: early on, Neimen residents made a living weaving bamboo baskets, supplying banana farmers in Qishan for export packing. When cardboard boxes seized the market in the 1960s, the bamboo-weaving industry declined, just as banquet catering (_bàn zhuō_) was taking off — so large numbers of craftsmen switched to the kitchen.[^8] The other, from local officials and cultural historians: Neimen has many temples and parade troupes (_zhèn tóu_); the Song Jiang Battle Array alone is a local trademark, and every festival required feeding large numbers of participants — this recurring need to "provide meals" (_bàn huǒ shí_) gave rise to professional head banquet chefs.[^9] Whether it was the bamboo-weaving pivot or the temple-festival feeding, both threads point to the same thing: this land forced people to develop a trade lived by hand skill.

At its peak, Neimen's roughly 14,000 residents included about 150 head banquet chef teams — nearly one in five households made a living from banquet catering, with simultaneous capacity to handle over 20,000 tables.[^2] This was the densest concentration of head banquet chefs in all of Taiwan. (This figure comes from research compiled in master's theses by Zhang Yuxin (2007) and Tang Yuning (2016) at I-Shou University, not a recent census.[^2])

This craft is passed down through apprenticeship. Once a kitchen assistant (_shuǐ jiǎo_) accumulates enough experience, they can graduate; second and third chefs then set up their own shops. Early on, through a "braised pig trotter" (_tāng zhū jiǎo_) style of master-apprentice succession (master takes disciple, disciple later takes their own), banquet-catering skills spread across Taiwan generation by generation.[^8]

The key step that turned Neimen from a group of craftsmen into an industry happened in 1976: Xue Qing-ji and several partners (including Deng Zheng-ping, plus a butcher and a vegetable vendor) formed a "four-in-one" company, integrating ingredient supply, cooking, and table-and-chair rental. From then on, banquet catering became a business with a complete supply chain — ingredients, cooking, tables and chairs, all handled in one package.[^8]

Today, Xue Meng-hui estimates Neimen has only about 30 to 40 head banquet chefs left, roughly one-quarter of the peak.[^8] The kingdom remains, but the people are scattering.

## Three Hundred Years Ago, Account Books Already Recorded "Hiring the Head Chef to Banquet"

Many people think _pān-toh_ (roadside banquets) only appeared in Taiwan after the war. Not so. Its roots can be traced all the way back to the Qing dynasty.

The _Taiwan Prefecture Gazetteer_ (compiled by Chen Wen-da, completed 1720), from the late Kangxi era of the Qing, already recorded that Taiwanese "whenever a family has a celebration or a seasonal festival, they must feast guests lavishly." By the Daoguang era, the _Changhua County Gazetteer_ (compiled by Chou Hsi, 1835) described banquets with "delicacies exhausting mountains and seas"[^1].

In 1902, early in the Japanese colonial period, an essay titled "Miscellaneous Notes on Banquets and Dishes" formally included the term "_pān-toh_" in the documentary record, explaining clearly: "Setting up tables, preparing food and drink, hosting a banquet is called '_pān-toh_'."[^10] This article came from the _Taiwan Customs Chronicle_: a monthly journal published by the Taiwan Customs Research Society (note: this is a different institution from the contemporaneous "Temporary Taiwan Old Customs Investigation Society," often confused with it)[^10].

So when exactly did this _pān-toh_ business begin?

Associate Research Fellow Tseng Pin-tsang of the Institute of Taiwan History, Academia Sinica, based on Qing-era account books and diaries, points out that Qing-era Taiwanese already commissioned professional chefs to handle banquets for weddings, funerals, and sacrificial rites[^11]. The evidence lies in the account books: for instance, the late-Qing Wufeng Lin family ledgers clearly record entries like "hired the '_tsóng-phòo_' (head chef/contractor) to banquet, how much per table"[^11]. Tseng infers that this custom of commissioning professional chefs for banquets can be traced back to the Kangxi period[^11]. Tracing further back, the origins of _pān-toh_ lie in Minnan and Guangdong: the banquet culture traditionally called "_bān-jiǔ_" or "_dào-huì_" in the Fujian and Guangzhou regions crossed the sea to Taiwan with Qing-era migrants[^12].

However, from the Qing dynasty up to the 1960s (the 50th year of the Republic), _pān-toh_ looked very different from today. Back then, cities relied on restaurants to provide outside catering, while rural areas mostly had to find villagers with slightly better cooking skills to moonlight, with limited dishes, and often the host family prepared the ingredients themselves. Wealthier households would have dedicated "_cháu-hā-bú_" (kitchen helpers) to manage things[^13]. True professionalization only began around the 1960s. When villagers discovered "moonlighting as a banquet cook pays better than farming," this craft gradually turned professional, peaking in the 1970s and 80s[^13].

> **💡 Did you know:** _Pān-toh_ is pronounced **pān-toh** in Taiwanese, and the head chef is called **tsóng-phòo-sai**. The character "師" (master/craftsman) is an honorific for professional artisans in Taiwanese folk custom[^14] — just like "_thóo-tsuí-sai_" (plasterer) and "_ba̍k-sai_" (carpenter), it's evidence that this craft was respected as an "art."

## The Sequence of Twelve Dishes Holds an Entire System of Invisible Knowledge

If the discipline of the kitchen crew is the backbone of a banquet, what follows is its soul.

A standard banquet typically runs twelve to fourteen courses, arranged with a clear narrative arc: cold appetizers arrive first to whet the appetite while guests settle in. Next come soups to warm the stomach. Then the climax — the most luxurious seafood or meat: lobster, _hong-kam_ (red crab / mud crab), Buddha Jumps Over the Wall — all appear here, calibrated to each table's budget in "common" and "premium" tiers. After the peak, a palate-cleansing soup is served, followed by rich, heavy flavors — dried bamboo shoots with braised pork belly or pork ribs — to rekindle the appetite. The meal closes with a final chicken soup, then dessert and fruit[^15]. (This serving sequence reflects the long-standing consensus of head banquet chefs and food media.)

Worth noting: lobster is not a "traditional" banquet dish. It only took center stage after the Ten Major Construction Projects and the economic takeoff of the 1960s. In earlier decades, banquet tables more commonly featured stir-fried rice noodles, poached chicken, celery squid soup, and meatball soup — for many people, a banquet was one of the few occasions they could eat meat[^16]. Even the grandest dish, Buddha Jumps Over the Wall, was originally called "Full Fortune and Longevity" (_hok-siû-choân_), originating from a Fuzhou official's family banquet in the Qing Guangxu era[^17].

But the true uniqueness of the banquet lies behind the dishes: they are embedded in a **cosmology of commensality**. Research from the National Museum of Taiwan History categorizes banquet commensality into three types — the analytical skeleton for understanding the banquet's "soul"[^15]:

The first is **human–deity commensality**. Seasonal festival banquets are where people and deities share the offerings; Taiwanese call this "eating the deity's blessings" (_i-sîn chia̍h-hok_): what goes down the throat is divine protection[^15]. The second is **human–ghost commensality**. Ancestral association gatherings, ritual corporate feasts, and the Ghost Festival universal salvation banquets are where people dine with ancestors and "good brothers," honoring forebears and establishing harmony with the other world[^15]. The third is the familiar **host–guest commensality**: life-ritual banquets celebrating life's passages, and association banquets cementing social bonds[^15]. A single banquet may span the human, divine, and ghost realms simultaneously.

This is why the head banquet chef is also the host family's **folk customs consultant**. In an interview with _The Reporter_, an industry insider described the role: "How to prepare a full-moon ceremony? How to arrange the offerings? Which foods are taboo for a given occasion? They know best."[^5]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> It's easy to think of a banquet as just "a bigger meal," but that framing misses what matters most. What a banquet really sells is **a mapping of timing to meaning** — which life moment calls for which tier of banquet, which dish is auspicious, inauspicious, or taboo in which setting. This mapping exists in no cookbook; it lives in the head chef's mind, in the muscle memory of one execution after another. When we later see "banquet dishes enter five-star hotels," remember: hotels can buy the recipes, but they cannot buy this mapping. What is being lost is the latter.

What does this mapping look like in concrete terms? Folklore scholar Chang Yun-shu's compilation reveals its granularity[^18]: a full-moon banquet uses a whole chicken, signifying "completeness" and symbolizing perfection (though the true core of the one-month celebration is actually sesame oil rice, sesame oil chicken, and red-dyed eggs). Funeral banquets must have an odd number of courses — seven or nine — and the table includes a piece of "triangular pork" (_sann-kak-bah_) with a missing corner; the shape's imperfection mirrors the bereaved family's grief, while lotus seeds, bitter melon, and pineapple are avoided for their homophonic or symbolic associations. Wedding banquets must include chicken (establishing the household), fish (surplus/abundance), braised pork belly (promotion), and pig stomach (wishing the bride a "big belly" / pregnancy), while the sticky rice with red crab must use roe-heavy female crabs to invoke "early birth of a noble son"[^18].

> **⚠️ Contested View:** Several circulating banquet taboos lack folkloric basis. For example, "funeral banquets must have _gua bao_" — _gua bao_ ("tiger bites pig") is actually a year-end banquet (Weiya, lunar 12/16) custom, symbolizing biting down on a year's bad luck, and has nothing to do with funerals. Another: "housewarming banquets avoid meatballs because 'ball' (_oân_) sounds like 'finished' (_oân-liáu_)," a claim with thin evidence that directly contradicts the custom of serving tangyuan at housewarmings to symbolize completeness[^19]. Writing about banquets means verifying even the taboos — because an error damages the credibility of this entire knowledge system.

The other face of the soul appears after the feast ends. When the banquet is over, the head chef combines the remnants of each dish into a "leftover medley soup" (also called mixed vegetable soup), distributing it to the neighbors who helped out[^20]. This is not mere leftover disposal; it is an act of cherishing blessings and giving thanks: _Taiwan Panorama_ describes it as a symbol of reciprocity[^20]. From start to finish, a banquet is the whole village's affair — the host family never does it alone. And the leftover medley soup itself is now on the verge of disappearing[^20].

## The "Heisong Grand Hotel" on Auspicious Lunar Calendar Days

Older-generation masters often use presidential terms to track the rise and fall of the industry.

The 12 years from 1988 to 2000, when Lee Teng-hui served as president, are regarded as the golden age of roadside banquets. It wasn't just the era of the traditional "eight celebrations and one funeral" (engagement, wedding, full-month celebration, return visit, business opening, birthday banquet, housewarming, funeral); it was an era when even a child earning a doctorate or winning money from pigeon racing called for a banquet[^21]. Whenever an auspicious lunar calendar day arrived, temple forecourts and roadsides were filled with "Heisong Grand Hotels." The name's origin is distinctly Taiwanese: early canvas tents were often provided by beverage companies, bearing advertisements for Heisong Sarsaparilla; over time, "Heisong Grand Hotel" became a synonym for roadside banquets[^21].

Just how prosperous those days were, Hsueh Meng-hui remembers most clearly through the work logs left by his father, Hsueh Ching-chi.

> **✦** 「The entire calendar was filled; 25,000 tables in a year.」——Hsueh Meng-hui recalling his father Hsueh Ching-chi's work logs[^21]

In Hsueh Ching-chi's era, even during slow months, there were still an average of 1,000 tables[^21]. By contrast, in Hsueh Meng-hui's own pre-pandemic period, a big month reaching 500 tables was cause for quiet celebration, and the yearly total was only a few thousand[^21]: in a single generation, the scale had shrunk several times over.

The turning point fell around 2003 (the third year of Chen Shui-bian's administration). Taiwan's industries moved westward en masse, and SME clients disappeared one by one. Some long-time clients hadn't ordered a banquet for two years running; upon inquiry, it turned out their companies had folded[^21]. The twilight of the golden age began with one unconnected phone call after another.

## Forty Counts as Young: A Generation That Is Breaking

An industry can shrink due to economic conditions and wait for the next cycle. But what roadside banquets face is something harder to reverse: **a generational rupture in succession**.

Master chef Li Chun-hsiang from New Taipei puts it bluntly: "For roadside banquet chefs, 40 counts as young — mostly only those with family connections take it up. You can't find 2 out of 10 chefs in their 30s; the kitchen assistants are universally aging."[^22] Another master chef, Chiang Yi-yung, offers a more concrete observation: only about 2% of culinary school graduates end up in roadside banquets.[^23] The reason isn't hard to understand: "For a lunch banquet, you have to leave at 3:30 AM — long hours, hot stoves."[^23] Given a choice, young people would rather work in air-conditioned restaurants.

Talent is draining away, and skills are breaking with it. The older generation of masters did everything themselves — from slaughtering pigs and chickens to making pudding and baking cakes. Now with outsourced frozen foods, apprentices in the kitchen can't learn the complete craft, and those most labor-intensive "hand-passed dishes" are disappearing one by one.[^24].

The legendary "chicken-pig stomach-softshell turtle" from the film _The Master Chef_ is the perfect example — stuffing a softshell turtle into a free-range chicken, then stuffing the whole chicken into a pig stomach, slow-braised for three hours, a process so elaborate almost no one is willing to do it.[^25] The "chestnut chicken" that was popular 40 years ago, young chefs no longer know how to make; others like deboned whole eel, bag chicken, and five-willow branches are also on the verge of being lost.[^24] (Note: sealed meat and red crab sticky rice, though labor-intensive, remain common today and are not among the lost dishes.) The person who has left the most records of these dishes is Huang Wan-ling, the "godmother of Taiwanese cuisine" — she served as the culinary consultant for the film _The Master Chef_, spending over a decade traveling the countryside to seek out and preserve those vanishing Taiwanese dishes.[^25].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> Notice there's an unspoken choice here. The market has actually given the roadside banquet industry an easy path: use frozen meal kits, use semi-finished products, and you can still put 12 dishes on the table — most guests can't tell the difference. Take this path, and the industry can survive longer. But the cost is that those hand-passed dishes, which can only be learned through oral instruction and hands-on teaching between master and apprentice, will quietly die on this road — not because no one likes eating them, but because no one needs to learn them anymore. Crafts are rarely eliminated by something new; more often, they are bypassed bit by bit by "good enough."

## The Longest Winter

If the generational gap is a chronic illness, then the 2020 pandemic was an acute crisis.

_The Reporter_ interviewed nearly ten head chefs (_zǒngpùshī_) across northern and southern Taiwan at the time; the consensus reached was that the number of catering tables had shrunk by at least 90% starting from the Lunar New Year (this was a consensus from interviews, not official statistics) [^26]. For Xue Meng-hui, over 500 tables were canceled in just the second and third months of the lunar calendar; for second-generation descendants of _bando_ families who were used to wielding large spatulas for stir-frying massive pots, they had to switch to small soup ladles during that period, portioning out individual lunch boxes just to survive [^26].

Official figures also failed to tell the whole story. According to statistics from the Ministry of Economic Affairs, the revenue of the "Catering and Group Meal Contracting Industry" in April 2020 decreased by 32.3% year-on-year, with monthly revenue at approximately NT$2.5 billion [^27]. However, this figure must be read with caution: it was primarily dragged down by airline catering (cloud kitchens), and a vast number of outdoor _bando_ banquets have no business registration and fall outside the scope of statistics—therefore, for outdoor _bando_, this figure actually **underestimates** the impact they suffered [^27].

```tw-stat

32.3% | YoY decrease in Catering and Group Meal Contracting Industry revenue | Ministry of Economic Affairs, April 2020
2% | Culinary graduates entering the catering industry | Observation by Chiang Yi-yung
Source: *The Reporter* head chef interviews (90% reduction in tables) + Ministry of Economic Affairs statistics (32.3% YoY decrease in Catering and Group Meal Contracting Industry in April 2020) + Chiang Yi-yung's observations on the proportion of culinary graduates entering the industry [^23][^26][^27]
```

Beyond the pandemic, northern _bando_ banquets face an even more structural dilemma: space. Li Chun-hsiang describes how _bando_ in the north must first close roads and apply for right-of-way, and they are frequently reported by neighbors: "Even if we are just deep-frying something, nearby residents will start cursing at us, threatening to report us to the Environmental Protection Bureau for air pollution. In extreme cases, people have even thrown things from upstairs to drive us away." [^28] Furthermore, community centers and elementary school auditoriums in Taipei City almost never lend their spaces out for banquets [^28]. In high-priced urban areas, it is becoming increasingly difficult for outdoor _bando_ to find even a single patch of land where they can legally set up tables.

Are all these pressures strictly bad? Cultural worker Yen Chen-yu has a different perspective. He believes the pandemic was both a blow and a stimulus, forcing head chefs to improve hygiene standards and seriously consider transformation [^29]. But the deepest fear among the interviewed head chefs is something else: "We aren't just worried about this year; we are more worried that people will get into the habit of not hiring us (for banquets)." [^29] The conclusion from _The Reporter_ is a sober one: _bando_ is tightly linked to the economy, and it will be difficult for it to recover in the short term [^29].

## The Dishes Remain, but the Soul is Fading

But if the story stops here, it would be profoundly mistaken.

Looking back from 2025, _bando_ is not simply dying in a one-way process. It is actually **diverging**—as "dishes" and "brands," it is moving upward; as a "village-wide ritual," it is sinking downward.

The upward branch is gaining incredible momentum. The _Michelin Guide_ has incorporated _bando_ dishes into its formal discourse, with three restaurants specializing in banquet cuisine—Ah-Hsia Hotel, Hsin Hsin Restaurant, and Tung Shang Taiwanese Cuisine—all receiving Bib Gourmand recognition [^30]. In Taipei, the Palais de Chine Hotel launched a "Taiwanese Banquet" series helmed by Chef Lin Ming-tsan, priced at NT$22,800 per table plus a 10% service charge, claiming to be the first five-star hotel in Taiwan to formally pay tribute to _bando_ culture [^31]. The younger generation is also taking the baton: in August 2025, TaiwanPlus reported on the subject under the title "Bando Is Back," showing how a new generation is driving a _bando_ revival through a model of "second-generation marketing × first-generation craftsmanship"—such as tofu master Huang Mao-yuan and his daughter Huang Chia-yu, or Master A-Long and his son A-Xiang [^32]. The English report stated: "Once fading from Taiwan's cultural fabric, this legendary banquet tradition is making a dynamic comeback..." [^32]

Even interest from overseas is flowing back. At the restaurant Good To Eat in Emeryville, California, Taiwanese chef Tony Tung (who, along with her partner Angie Lin, is a wife-wife team) treats _bando_ as a love letter to Taiwan, naming her menu _jan ba bae_ (_bando_) [^33]. On January 4, 2025, she invited the Neimen master Master A-Càn to Taipei to host a traditional banquet for over 400 people, attracting California foodies to fly all the way back to Taiwan specifically for the experience [^33].

```tw-versus
Open-air Bando (Shrinking) | Refined Bando (Ascending)
Red and white canvas tents, set up by roadsides or temple entrances | Five-star hotel ballrooms, Michelin tables
Starting at 3:30 AM; out of ten 30-year-old masters, two cannot be found | NT$22,800 per table at Palais de Chine; second-generation marketing takes over
A village-wide ritual | A pilgrimage of cultural experience
Source: Michelin Guide, Palais de Chine Hotel, and TaiwanPlus reports cited in this text, as well as observations on master manpower [^22][^23][^30][^31][^32]
```

When you look at these two branches together, the shape of the divergence becomes clear. What is being revived are the "dishes" and the "brands"—they can enter five-star hotels, Michelin tables, and restaurants in California; what is being lost is the body of ritual knowledge. A five-star hotel ballroom can replicate a pot of _fo tiao qiang_ (Buddha Jumps Over the Wall), but it cannot replicate the entire unspoken understanding of "the whole village mobilizing, the master serving as a folk consultant, and the final dishes being distributed to neighbors." Scarcity has turned _bando_ cuisine into a form of cultural pilgrimage, with California diners willing to fly halfway around the world to Taiwan for one meal—but the object of that pilgrimage is losing the habitat it relies on to survive: those badlands that nurture masters, those back-to-back temple fairs, and those villages where everyone calls upon their neighbors to help.

Air-conditioned restaurants can sustain the dishes, but they cannot sustain the soul.

## That Well-Worn Farmer's Almanac

Back to Hsueh Ching-chi's farmer's almanac diary, filled cover to cover with writing.

It is the object Hsueh Meng-hui treasures most, and also the most authentic archaeological site of this industry[^21]. Those dense orders in the diary — celebration banquets for winning pigeon races, thanksgiving banquets for earning doctorates, peace banquets for Mazu's birthday at the temple — record a whole set of conventions that Taiwan people once collectively recognized: which moments were worth everyone putting down their work to gather together, what should be eaten at that moment, who should preside, and how to properly bid farewell.

The cuisine has already proven it can enter five-star hotels, fly to California, and be Michelin-recognized. But that set of tacit understandings written in that farmer's almanac — which moment calls for what kind of banquet table, which dish is auspicious or inauspicious for which occasion, after the banquet which neighbors who helped out should receive the leftover portions — how many people still remember, how many can still carry it on?
