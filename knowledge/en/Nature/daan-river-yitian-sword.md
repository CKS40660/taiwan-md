> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan is home to a tree that stands 84.1 meters tall—roughly equivalent to a 28-story building—making it the tallest known tree in East Asia[^1]. Named "Da'an River Yitianjian," this _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan cypress) grows in a deep valley at the source of the Da'an River, approximately 1,650 meters above sea level, and is estimated to be about 700 to 800 years old[^2]. During the 2023 Lunar New Year, "The Tree Finder" team trekked upstream for seven days, enduring night temperatures that dropped to -2°C, before climbing into its canopy to drop a measuring tape and record this figure[^3]. Most counterintuitively: on an island swept by typhoons every year with fragile geology prone to landslides and flooding, such height should not theoretically exist. It has reached 84 meters and survived for seven centuries precisely because it is hidden in a valley nearly inaccessible to humans. This same "inaccessibility" allowed it to escape the massive logging eras of the Japanese colonial period and the post-war years, and it took us nearly a decade of scanning the entire island with LiDAR to finally discover it this year[^4].

Early in the 2023 Lunar New Year, above an unnamed valley deep within the Xueshan Range. Several people clung to the canopy of a Taiwan cypress, slowly lowering a roll of measuring tape from the top toward the distant forest floor. The moment the tape stopped, the number read **84.1 meters**[^3].

It is the tallest tree on record in Taiwan and currently the tallest known tree in East Asia[^1]. But what truly makes one pause is how long it has been hiding. This tree has stood in that valley for at least seven hundred years[^2], enduring cycles of logging from the Qing dynasty, the Japanese colonial period, and the post-war era, as well as the typhoons that sweep across Taiwan every year—and it was not until this year that we finally learned it was there.

## 84.1 Meters, Taller Than the Instruments Guessed

Measuring the height of a tree sounds like it should be one of the simplest tasks. However, the tallest trees in the world happen to be the most difficult to measure accurately.

The "Tree Finder: Giant Tree Map Project" utilizes airborne LiDAR: aircraft carry lasers that scan through forests, using the reflected point clouds to calculate canopy height models for every patch of woodland[^5]. In the data, this giant tree, numbered 55214, was estimated to be 79.5 meters tall[^6]. This height alone would have been enough to place it among the top tier in Taiwan.

But lasers have their blind spots. They fire from the sky downward, hitting the outermost reflective surfaces of the canopy blocked by leaves rather than the true highest point; steep slopes, obstructions, and insufficient point density can all cause underestimation. To determine exactly how tall a tree is, the most accurate method to this day remains almost clumsily primitive: a person climbs up and drops a measuring tape from the treetop to the ground to measure it directly. This method is called "tape-drop"[^7].

When team members actually climbed to the top of 55214 and lowered the tape, the figure was 84.1 meters—a full 4.6 meters higher than the laser's guess[^6]. In other words, the tallest living thing on this island even deceived instruments stationed in the sky, which underestimated it by nearly five meters.

```tw-figure
79.5 meters → 84.1 meters
From airborne LiDAR predicted height to actual height via tree-climbing tape-drop: The instrument underestimated it by 4.6 meters.
Source: Hsu et al., Frontiers in Forests and Global Change, 2026 (DOI: 10.3389/ffgc.2026.1746112)
```

This 4.6-meter discrepancy serves almost as a footnote for this entire story. The project team compared 22 giant trees across Taiwan exceeding 40 meters that had been climbed and measured manually; the average difference (RMSE) between laser and tape was 4.55 meters[^8]. The taller and more deeply hidden a tree is, the more likely instruments are to underestimate it. The tallest one is often the one most unwilling to be seen clearly.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> It is easy to think of "measurement" as something neutral and objective, assuming that as long as instruments are advanced enough, everything can be mastered. But the numbers 84.1 versus 79.5 remind us of one thing: no matter how precise a laser is, it ultimately requires a person to climb to the treetop and drop a tape to reach a final verdict. Because _Yitianjian_ (Heaven Sword) is the tallest, it is also the hardest to calculate accurately. The highest life on this island was confirmed using the most ancient of ways.

## An Island Where Giant Trees Shouldn't Exist

Zooming out to look at the entire island, the very existence of Yitianjian is a mystery.

Taiwan is not a place suited for growing ultra-tall trees. Every year, three to five typhoons strike directly; giant trees on high mountain ridges bear the brunt of it, being snapped by wind, struck by lightning, or uprooted entirely. Combined with young geology and steep slopes, heavy rains trigger large-scale landslides. In such an environment, for a tree to grow taller than 80 meters, it requires not only several hundred years of time but also several hundred years of luck—luck to avoid being snapped by wind, being logged, or being swept away by landslides[^9].

Consequently, ultra-tall trees in Taiwan are extraordinarily rare. Currently, there are only a few giant trees exceeding 70 meters in all of Taiwan, and they all belong to the same species: _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan cypress)[^10]. Why is it Taiwan cypress, rather than the cypress (_Chamaecyparis formosensis_) that most people associate with "sacred trees"? There is a piece of common knowledge hidden here that most people have reversed.

The "sacred trees" in our memory are almost all red cypress or Hinoki cypress, such as the Alishan Sacred Trees or the Lala Mountain Sacred Trees—thick, ancient, and cordoned off for worship. However, red cypress only reaches a maximum height of about 65 meters[^11]. The reason they survived to become sacred trees today is based on a somewhat cruel logic: red cypress is susceptible to fungal infections, often leaving the heartwood hollowed out like a honeycomb. Because the timber has no practical value, no one bothered to log them during the logging era, which inadvertently saved their lives[^11]. Taiwan cypress is different; its trunk is straight and its heartwood is not hollow, making it premium construction material, with a growth potential of up to approximately 90 meters[^12]. It grows the tallest, but because of that, it was also the most "worth" logging.

![Foliage and straight form of Taiwan cypress](/article-images/nature/taiwania-kenpei.webp)
_The foliage and straight form of Taiwan cypress: The trunk is straight and the heartwood is not hollow, making it premium construction material; this also made it the most "worth" logging during the logging era. Photo: KENPEI / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0_

So why did the tallest Taiwan cypress survive? The answer lies not in the tree itself, but in where it stands.

## Concealment Equals Survival

_Yitianjian_ grows in a deep valley at the source of the Da'an River, at an altitude of approximately 1,650 meters[^13]. To reach it, one must trek upstream along the Da'an River for about 20 kilometers, crossing powerful currents and climbing steep cliffs prone to rockfalls and landslides—descending over a thousand meters after ascending nearly a thousand meters. This is a route that even experienced mountaineers might struggle to navigate[^14]. Researcher Hsu Chia-chun describes the habitat of such giant trees: "The locations where these giant trees are found are environments that even experienced mountaineers find difficult to reach."[^15]

It is precisely this "inaccessibility" that has served as its life insurance.

From the Japanese colonial period through the post-war era, Taiwan underwent successive waves of large-scale logging; cypress and _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan cypress) from Alishan, Taipingshan, and Baxianshan were transported down mountains in massive quantities. However, logging machinery has its limits—it can only harvest where roads are built and timber can be extracted. The sheer cliffs and deep valleys at the source of the Da'an River were completely unreachable by cableways or trucks, so this 84-meter giant was left behind. It hid deeply enough to survive.

Hsu Chia-chun explains this logic precisely: "The location of _Yitianjian_ shares similar characteristics with other discovered giant trees: it exists within an unlogged primary forest and is situated in a valley that can avoid typhoons."[^16] This single observation unlocks two mysteries: height and lifespan. The valley topography shields the tree from the direct impact of typhoons, preventing it from being snapped by the wind like trees on a ridge; meanwhile, the unlogged primary forest allowed it the opportunity to accumulate time into height.

![Cloud forest in Shei-Pa National Park](/article-images/nature/shei-pa-cloud-forest.webp)
_The cloud forest of Shei-Pa National Park: A high-humidity environment with afternoon fog and an annual rainfall of approximately 3,000 mm is key to allowing Taiwan cypress to transport water to its 80-meter canopy. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

Scientifically, the ability of Taiwan cypress to grow this tall relies on the convergence of three factors. First is the high humidity of the cloud forest: Taiwan cypress naturally distributes in the cloud belt at altitudes of 1,500 to 2,400 meters with an annual rainfall of about 3,000 mm[^17]. The afternoon fog allows leaves to absorb moisture directly, solving the massive hydraulic pressure problem of transporting water from the roots to an 80-meter canopy. Second is the combination of valley topography for typhoon protection and the pioneer advantage in landslide areas: Taiwan cypress seeds are tiny and can disperse long distances via wind; seedlings often land on landslide sites or fallen logs in valleys. As a light-loving pioneer species, once a landslide opens a forest gap and sunlight pours in, it quickly surges upward[^18]. Third is concealment equals survival: the inaccessible terrain allowed it to escape human saws.

Red cypress survives logging because of hollow trunks; Taiwan cypress survives because of sheer valley walls—the two oldest types of giant trees on this island used two completely different methods to evade the same era of logging.

```tw-stat

Approx. 8.5 m | Girth | Requires several people hugging to encircle
Est. 700–800 years | Age | Estimated from girth; not dated via core sampling
941 trees | Giant trees >65m located in Taiwan | As of the end of 2022
Source: Field measurements by The Tree Finder team (height 84.1 m, girth approx. 8.5 m) and age estimates/giant tree location data cited in this article[^1][^6][^19][^20]
```

Regarding those seven hundred years, one figure must be clarified. _Yitianjian_ is "estimated to be about 700 to 800 years old"—this number was calculated based on the approximately 8.5-meter girth and the known growth rate of Taiwan cypress, without actual core sampling for dendrochronology[^19]. Some international reports in 2026 even claim it is "nearly a thousand years old"[^20]; this older figure may stem from a different estimation method. Estimating age by girth carries uncertainty: the Shuishan Divine Tree in Alishan was once estimated to be 2,700 years old, but after core sampling by the Forestry Bureau, it was corrected to 1,081 years—an error of over 1,600 years[^21]. Therefore, regarding _Yitianjian_, a more responsible statement is: it is estimated to be seven or eight hundred years old, and potentially even older, but until someone actually drills into its rings, this remains a number accompanied by a question mark.

## The Tree Finders, and the Eyes That Spent a Lifetime Searching for Trees

_Yitianjian_ was "found." And the story of finding it begins on an afternoon when a pair of shoes broke.

In 1994, Hsu Chia-chun walked the Nenggao Yuehling for the first time. By the fourth day, her shoes were ruined. She leaned against the Chilai Dam, exhausted, but her gaze was suddenly captured by a giant tree in the valley across from her—a _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan cypress). Looking at the towering silhouette of that tree, she said that amidst her sense of awe, she suddenly found the strength to keep going[^22]. At that moment, she did not know that this single image would guide her through the rest of her life.

That tree was actually a different one. _Yitianjian_ is located at the source of the Da'an River; it is a different place and a different Taiwan cypress. But the one across from Chilai Dam in 1994 was the starting point for the act of "seeing." Hsu Chia-chun later studied botany at National Taiwan University, earned her PhD in Amsterdam, and joined the Forestry Research Institute to study epiphytes in the forest canopy, becoming someone whose entire life would be spent interacting with trees high above the ground[^23].

The truly systematic search for "Taiwan's tallest tree" began in 2014. That year, Hsu Chia-chun's team visited the "Three Sisters," a group of Taiwan cypresses in the Qilan Conservation Area, officially embarking on the quest to find giant trees[^24]. Since 2014, they have successively located, climbed, and measured 22 giant trees exceeding 40 meters using measuring tapes[^8]. But searching one by one on foot was too slow. The island is too large and contains too many deep valleys; as Hsu Chia-chun herself put it: "Many trees are in places where no humans live; just cutting grass to clear a path takes over 10 days."[^25]

The turning point came from a batch of laser data. In the 2010s, following Typhoon Morakot, the Ministry of the Interior successively acquired airborne LiDAR scans of all of Taiwan; starting in 2016, Wang Chi-kuei's team from the Department of Surveying and Geo-informatics at National Cheng Kung University launched a second large-scale scan[^26]. In 2019, Hsu Chia-chun and Wang Chi-kuei officially formed the "Tree Finder—Giant Tree Map Project," turning laser point clouds into a systematic map of giant tree hotspots[^27].

```tw-timeline
1994 | The afternoon shoes broke | While walking Nenggao Yuehling, a Taiwan cypress in the valley across Chilai Dam became the starting point for "seeing."
2014 | Starting the search for the tallest | Visited Qilan's "Three Sisters"; since then, 22 giant trees >40m have been climbed and measured.
2016 | Second scan launched | Wang Chi-kuei's team at NCKU re-scanned Taiwan's forests using airborne LiDAR.
2019 | Tree Finders formed | Hsu Chia-chun × Wang Chi-kuei officially form the Giant Tree Map Project, combining laser tech + citizen science.
2020 | Collective interpretation | Hundreds of citizens interpreted over 50,000 LiDAR profiles online, filtering out tens of thousands of algorithmic errors.
2022 | An island-wide giant tree map | Located 941 giant trees exceeding 65 meters across Taiwan.
2023 | Yitianjian unsheathed | A 7-day stream trek during Lunar New Year confirmed 55214 = 84.1m, the tallest tree in East Asia.
2026 | Published in international journals | The measurement process featured in Frontiers, with simultaneous reports by CNN and Mongabay.
Source: Interviews with the Tree Finder Giant Tree Map Project and Hsu Chia-chun, as well as Ministry of the Interior airborne LiDAR and NCKU Wang Chi-kuei team scan records[^8][^22][^24][^25][^26][^27]
```

Laser technology alone was not enough. Among the candidate points pre-screened by algorithms, the error rate was absurdly high; over 90% of the "tall trees" flagged by machines were actually miscalculations[^28]. Thus, in 2020, the project did something very Taiwanese: they uploaded over 50,000 LiDAR profiles to a citizen science platform, allowing hundreds of citizens to interpret them online. They relied on the eyes of the crowd to brush away tens of thousands of algorithmic errors, one profile at a time[^28]. To encourage participation, the platform even designed progression badges; those who found the tallest tree could even earn naming rights for the giant tree[^29].

Lasers scan the entire island, citizens' eyes filter through every profile, and finally, humans climb to the treetops to finalize measurements with tapes. _Yitianjian_ is the final number produced by this entire technological chain. By the end of 2022, this "Taiwan Giant Tree Map" had already marked 941 giant trees exceeding 65 meters[^30]. Number 55214 is one of those coordinates.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> "Finding the tallest tree on the island" requires more than just an extraordinary tree. It requires an aircraft that scanned the whole island, a set of laser algorithms, hundreds of strangers sitting before computers interpreting profiles, several Indigenous team members to lead the way, a group of scientists willing to trek upstream for seven days to climb a tree, and finally, a single measuring tape. _Yitianjian_ was only "seen" in 2023—but this "seeing" is the process of an entire island collectively rediscovering itself over the course of a decade.

## Seven Days of Drawing the Sword

On the second day of the 2023 Lunar New Year, The Tree Finder team set out.[^31]

Their target was No. 55214, a giant tree that had been marked on Hsu Chia-chun's exploration map for over half a year. Before departing, she wrote down an obsession that made the trip non-negotiable: "Even if I don't climb the tree, I will find a way to see 55214 with minimal gear."[^32] She was also aware that LiDAR is not infallible: "LiDAR data was originally scanned at a density of 2 points per meter, so there remains a very small probability that tall trees are located within 'gaps'."[^33] The tallest tree might happen to fall into a blind spot missed by the laser; no one could say for sure what they would see upon climbing up.

Regarding the perils of the journey, Hsu Chia-chun later described it: one must trek approximately 20 km upstream, risking being swept away while crossing the high-volume Da'an River, then climb a 1,500-meter ridge and traverse steep landslide areas; during the process, someone was even accidentally injured by falling rocks, before they finally saw that tall, slender giant tree in the distance.[^34] The entire journey took seven days, and after nightfall, temperatures once dropped to -2 degrees Celsius.[^3]

![Dabajian Mountain at the source of the Da'an River](/article-images/nature/dabajianshan-alan.webp)
_The Da'an River originates from the west side of Dabajian Mountain; Yitianjian is hidden deep within this part of the Xueshan Range, in a headwater valley that is almost unreachable by humans. Photo: Alan Wu / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

Seven days, -2 degrees, broken shoes, being hit by rocks, and crossing turbulent streams—this is the actual cost translated from the phrase "unreachable." The same reason Yitianjian has survived to this day is also what caused those measuring it immense hardship. At the moment a team member climbed into the canopy and lowered a tape measure to confirm the height of 84.1 meters, Hsu Chia-chun described it as a moment of both tension and joy, especially for those team members who had correctly guessed its true height beforehand.[^35]

It naturally took its place as Taiwan's tallest tree. Second place is the Ka-a-lang giant tree in the Zhuoshui River basin at approximately 82 meters; third is the Taoshan Divine Tree in Shei-Pa, at 79.1 meters.[^36] And Taiwan's top ten tallest trees are, without exception, all _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan cypress).[^37]

```tw-bars
The Three Tallest Trees in Taiwan (meters)
*Da'an River Yitianjian | 84.1 | Source of Da'an River, tallest in East Asia
Ka-a-lang Giant Tree | 82 | Zhuoshui River basin, estimated via drone with scale
Taoshan Divine Tree | 79.1 | Shei-Pa National Park
Source: Forestry and Nature Conservation Agency, Ministry of Agriculture; The Tree Finder Project
```

These three numbers side by side show that the gap is actually not large: 84.1, 82, and 79.1. But they uphold a fact that few countries can claim: Taiwan is one of the few known places in East Asia where giant trees exceeding 70 meters can grow.[^38] On an island hit by typhoons every year, these extra few meters of height were earned through centuries of concealment.

The area where Yitianjian is located is administratively managed at the junction of the Taichung Branch of the Forestry and Nature Conservation Agency, Ministry of Agriculture, and Shei-Pa National Park.[^39] The research was co-led by Hsu Chia-chun from the Forestry Research Institute and Wang Chi-kui from National Cheng Kung University, with funding provided by the Ministry of Agriculture and Yuen Foong Yu Consumer Products Co., Ltd.[^40] In June 2026, this process of searching and measuring was officially published in the international journal _Frontiers in Forests and Global Change_[^8], with simultaneous reports from CNN, Mongabay, and Sci.News, allowing this seven-hundred-year-old tree to be seen by the world for the first time.[^41]

## Three Names, and One Absent Name

This tree has more than one name. And how it was named is itself a quiet story that warrants a pause for reflection.

The oldest layer is the scientific name. The genus name for _Taiwania_ (Taiwan cypress) was established in 1906 by Japanese botanist Bunzo Hayata. The specimen was collected in 1904 from Wusongkeng in Nantou Prefecture by technician Konishi Seisho of the Governor-General's Office; Hayata published it at the Linnean Society in London, using the place name "Taiwan" to name it[^42]. This is a unique case in botanical taxonomy: _Taiwania_ is the only genus in the plant kingdom named after "Taiwan"[^43]. However, Taiwan cypress is not endemic to Taiwan; its natural distribution spans Taiwan, southwestern China, northern Myanmar, and Vietnam[^44]. What is truly unique to Taiwan is only that genus name. And that colonial-era naming process completely bypassed the knowledge systems of the Indigenous peoples who have inhabited this island for generations[^42].

![Japanese botanist Bunzo Hayata](/article-images/nature/bunzo-hayata-sasaki.webp)
_Japanese botanist Bunzo Hayata, who named this genus (Taiwania) after "Taiwan" at the Linnean Society in London in 1906. It is the only genus in the plant kingdom named after Taiwan. Photo: Sasaki / Wikimedia Commons, Public domain_

The second layer is a poetic name: "The tree that hits the moon." This is the Rukai name for the Taiwan cypress species. Standing at the foot of such a coniferous giant, looking up to find no canopy, one feels so small that this romantic, almost mythical name was born[^45]. However, this name belongs to the entire species: it is how the Rukai people refer to "Taiwan cypress" within their traditional territories around Dagu Lake in the south; it is neither the name of this specific tree, Yitianjian, nor is it Atayal[^46].

The third layer is "Yitianjian." During the 2013 expedition, the team passed through Dongyang Mountain and Xishi Mountain—known in the mountaineering community by the nicknames "Eastern Heretic and Western Poison"—and after descending into the Da'an River valley, they witnessed a vast forest of giant trees. The spectacle was as grand as the "Mount Hua Swordfight" (Huashan Lunjian) from Jin Yong's wuxia novels, where the world's greatest masters gather to decide who is number one under heaven[^47]. The tallest among them, 55214, was named Yitianjian. The name comes from _The Heaven Sword and Dragon Saber_, where "Yitian" (leaning on heaven) describes an extremely long sword[^48]. An incredibly long, sky-piercing, unrivaled sword perfectly corresponds to this Taiwan cypress that stands tall like a blade. From "Eastern Heretic and Western Poison" to "Mount Hua Swordfight" and finally to "Yitianjian," these are interconnected wuxia images, not chosen at random. The naming was completed only after the expedition returned; while in the mountains, it was still just called "55214"[^49]. The person who named it was an Indigenous team member from the tree-finding group. All public records simply refer to them as an "Indigenous team member," without leaving a name or tribal affiliation[^50].

Placing these three names side by side reveals a quiet tension. The scientific name comes from a Japanese colonial botanist, the poetic name from the Rukai people of the south, and the wuxia name from contemporary Han culture; all three layers have voices that were heard. Yet, this tree stands in the upstream core of the traditional territory of the Atayal Beshi group: the Beshi group calls themselves _Liyung-Painux_, meaning "rushing stream," and there are a total of 13 Beshi tribal settlements along the Da'an River[^51]. In currently available public data, no Atayal name can be found for this tree or the land where it stands[^52].

This is not to say such a name does not exist. The Atayal _gaga_ (oral tradition) explicitly includes "ecological knowledge," and the traditional system of understanding flora and fauna in the Da'an River basin is alive[^53]; it is just that the Indigenous name for this giant tree, located at an altitude of 1,650 meters in the extreme upper reaches of a traditional hunting ground, has not yet appeared in public literature. Perhaps because it is too deep, or too remote; perhaps because oral traditions do not naturally leave written place names for every single tree. Therefore, one can only write honestly here: it is not seen in current public data, rather than saying it "does not exist." Hsu Chia-chun herself emphasized the contribution of the Indigenous team members during interviews, stating that every expedition involved Indigenous participation, and their ability to survive in the mountains relied on the local knowledge of the Indigenous people regarding the forests[^54].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> A tree simultaneously possesses a colonizer's scientific name, a southern tribe's poetic name, and a modern person's wuxia name, yet it uniquely lacks the Indigenous name of the masters of the land beneath its feet. This gap does not require anyone to pass moral judgment. It merely reminds us: behind "naming" lies the question of who has the right to speak, whose knowledge is written into literature, and whose voices remain quiet in the story of this island. Leaving this silence as it is, allowing readers to think for themselves, is closer to the truth than rushing to provide an answer.

## After Seeing Them

Once it was known to be there, Taiwan began looking for ways to "see it clearly."

In March 2025, tree-climbing photography expert Steven Pearce from Tasmania, Australia, and his team completed a "life-sized portrait" of Yitianjian[^55]. Their method was clumsy yet persistent: the climbing experts slowly scaled the tree, taking photos one by one at every parallel height—approximately 120 photos in total—and then stitching them together grid by grid to create a complete image of the entire tree from root to crown[^56]. Pearce has previously photographed giant Eucalyptus trees in Australia and the United States; he stated in English that this was the most difficult tree-climbing photography expedition he had ever undertaken, and that Taiwan's forests and scenery are second to none in the world[^57].

The most moving aspect of that life-sized portrait is the scale. When an entire 84-meter tree is presented in full, a person standing at its base is as small as a comma in a line of text. We are accustomed to looking up at trees, but we rarely have the chance to see a whole tree at once. It was only at the moment of seeing the whole that one truly felt the weight of seven hundred years.

Even earlier, starting in 2021, the documentary 《神木之島》 (Island of Divine Trees) followed in the footsteps of Hsu Chia-chun, venturing into the depths of Xueshan and the Central Mountain Range to film these giant trees[^58]. The Taiwan Nature Conservation and Environmental Information Fund (TNF) also produced an official documentary recording the full exploration of Yitianjian, following the team through that journey of climbing 1,000 meters up and descending 1,700 meters[^59]:

But after seeing them, there remains an even harder question to answer: how much longer can these visible giants stand?

This is the other side of "invisibility equals survival." Being "unreachable" once protected Yitianjian from saws, but it cannot hide from the climate. Comparing LiDAR data taken ten years apart, approximately 5% of the 941 giant trees in Taiwan have already disappeared; Wang Chi-kuei from National Cheng Kung University issued a warning in English that, at this rate, existing giant trees may all wither away within the next one or two centuries[^61]. Most giant trees grow in geologically fragile valley environments, and as typhoon intensity and extreme rainfall increase, landslides can directly sweep away a tree that has stood for a millennium. The _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan Cypress) is itself listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN[^62].

However, while these giant trees are alive, they are also doing something great for this island. "The Tree Finder" team investigated the Giant Tree Valley where the Taoshan Divine Tree is located and estimated that the carbon storage per hectare is approximately 1,384 metric tons, approaching the carbon density of the giant tree forests in Tasmania, Australia, which are among the highest in the world[^63]. A forest hidden in a deep valley, which we have only just learned to see, is silently storing this much carbon. To see them is, in a sense, to see how much of Taiwan's mountain forests remains worth protecting.

## It Survived Until Today Because We Couldn't Reach It Before

Returning to that afternoon in 1994. With broken shoes, Hsu Chia-chun sat by the Chilai Dam, drawn to a _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ (Taiwan cypress) in the valley across from her; that attraction gave her the strength to descend. Thirty years later, she and a group of people scanned the entire island with lasers, used the eyes of citizen scientists to review 50,000 profile maps, trekked upstream for seven days, and lowered a tape measure from the top of another Taiwan cypress: 84.1 meters.

This small island, which we thought we had long since mapped out, still hides life that is ten times older than us and taller than any building. And it has survived until today precisely because we could not reach it before.

That "inaccessibility" was once its life insurance, allowing it to escape large-scale logging, dodge typhoons, and stand quietly in an uninhabited valley for seven hundred years. Now, using lasers, citizen science, tree climbing, and the guidance of Indigenous team members, we have finally crossed that barrier to see its full majesty for the first time. To "see" is a form of arrival; but what we must be careful of is not letting "seeing" become another form of harm.

A day trip to Yitianjian is nearly impossible. The seven-day journey, the -2°C nights, and the rushing streams serve as a natural hedge, keeping the influx of potential visitors at bay. Its best protection may still be that ancient state of being unreachable.

And now, above an unnamed deep valley at the source of the Da'an River, an 84-meter-tall green sword still quietly pierces the sky.
