# Hot Spring Culture: From War Trauma to Healing Empire

In March 1896, when Japanese rōnin Hirata Gengo built "Tengu-an" (天狗庵) by the Beitou Stream, this former policeman with a bullet wound in his knee could never have imagined that this humble inn, built to treat his own leg injury and athlete's foot, would open the curtain on Taiwan's hot spring civilization. Even less could he have foreseen that this industry, accidentally catalyzed by war trauma, would ultimately propel Taiwan into a healing empire boasting three of the world's mud hot springs and three of the world's submarine hot springs.

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's hot spring culture originated from military medical needs during the Japanese colonial era, evolving from an individual's chance discovery while seeking treatment into a world-class density distribution of 128 hot spring areas. Guanziling's mud hot springs and Green Island's submarine hot springs are both rare geological miracles found in only three locations worldwide. Taiwan-style hot springs further fuse Japanese healing traditions with local social culture, creating a unique "hot spring+" composite experience.

**Taiwan's hot springs are not a product of the tourism industry, but a civilizational miracle woven unexpectedly from war and healing.**

Today, Taiwan's 128 hot spring areas are distributed across the island at an astonishing density of one per 281 square kilometers. This density far surpasses that of hot spring powerhouse Japan (approximately 3 per 1,000 square kilometers). From Beitou's acidic sulfur springs to Guanziling's black mud baths, from Jiaoxi's "beauty springs" to Green Island's submarine hot springs, the people of Taiwan have created a unique "Taiwan-style hot spring civilization" — a distinctive experience blending Japanese ritual sensibility, Taiwanese human warmth, and modern urban convenience.

## War-Catalyzed Healing Empire

The true origins of Taiwan's hot spring development trace back to the Japanese military's medical crisis of 1895.

In the early days of Japanese rule, tropical diseases and medical shortages inflicted heavy casualties on the Japanese army. The Governor-General's Office urgently needed to establish a convalescence system for the military and began systematically surveying hot spring resources across the island. Every hot spring discovered was immediately subjected to water quality analysis and assessed for potential as a military sanatorium.

**Hirata Gengo was an accidental pioneer against this backdrop.** This former policeman, who had participated in the movement to overthrow the shogunate, came to Taiwan hoping to strike it rich mining gold. After his application was rejected and he was injured during exploration, athlete's foot flared up simultaneously. Unable to find a doctor even in Keelung, he heard of hot springs at the foot of Mount Datun and, with nothing to lose, headed to Beitou.

On November 25, 1895, Hirata Gengo lodged with a Taiwanese family surnamed Chen and began soaking in the stream to heal his wounds. A month later, recovered, he decided to open a humble hot spring inn here — Tengu-an — becoming Taiwan's first hot spring operator.

But what truly launched Beitou's hot springs was the military government's systematic investment. In August 1896, Matsumoto Kitarō, Finance Section Chief of the Taipei Military Government, built "Shōtō-en" (松濤園) exclusively for military officials' convalescence. This was followed by Hōyō-en (保養園) and Hokutō-kan (北投館), transforming Beitou from an individual's chance medical discovery into an officially certified convalescence base.

**This military-medical development model accidentally laid the quality genes of Taiwan's hot spring industry.** To meet military convalescence needs, the Japanese government imposed strict standards on water quality, facilities, and service — standards that continue to this day, becoming the competitive advantage of Taiwan's hot spring industry.

> **💡 Did You Know** Without war, Taiwan might not have today's hot spring culture. The historical entanglement of healing and killing created the purest space for physical and mental restoration.

## Earth's Exclusive Gifts to Taiwan

Taiwan's world-class hot spring status comes from extraordinarily fortunate geological conditions.

Situated at the boundary of the Eurasian and Philippine Sea plates, Taiwan possesses the world's highest density of hot spring distribution. 128 hot spring areas are distributed across 36,000 square kilometers of land, averaging one per 281 square kilometers — a figure far exceeding hot spring powerhouse Japan (approximately 3 per 1,000 square kilometers) and Iceland (approximately 1 per 1,000 square kilometers).

Even more astonishing is the rarity of the spring types. Within a north-south span of 400 kilometers, Taiwan lets you experience the vast majority of the Earth's hot spring types: from Beitou's acidic sulfate springs (pH 1.5–3) to Jiaoxi's alkaline sodium bicarbonate springs (pH 8–9), temperatures ranging from 40°C to 75°C, mineral compositions covering sulfur, sodium bicarbonate, chloride, sulfate, and other major types.

**Guanziling's mud hot springs are Taiwan's world-class signature.** Globally, only Sicily in Italy, Kagoshima in Japan, and Guanziling in Taiwan possess natural mud hot springs. Guanziling's gray-black spring water contains rich minerals and fine mud particles, with a spring temperature of approximately 75°C, praised during the Japanese era as "the world's most efficacious spring." This mud forms from the long-term interaction of the formation's gray-green rock with hot spring water, possessing unique therapeutic effects.

**Green Island's Asahi Hot Spring is another geological miracle.** Worldwide, only northern Italy, Japan's Yakushima Island in Kyushu, and Taiwan's Green Island have submarine hot springs. These springs well up directly from the intertidal zone, letting visitors soak while listening to the waves, enjoying a world-unique healing experience at sunrise.

> **📊 Data Source** According to 2024 statistics from the Central Geological Survey, Ministry of Economic Affairs, among Taiwan's 135 natural hot springs, 52% are sodium bicarbonate springs, 31% are sulfur springs, and 17% are other special spring types. Such diversity within the same land area is globally rare.

## From Zen to Entertainment: The Cultural Mutation of Taiwan-Style Hot Springs

The most captivating aspect of Taiwan's hot spring culture lies in the "Taiwanese evolution of Japanese genes."

Traditional Japanese hot springs emphasize the concept of "tōji" (湯治) — long-term soaking for medical effect, pursuing pure physical and mental healing. But Taiwanese have transformed hot springs into social platforms and family entertainment centers.

Taiwan-style hot spring resorts are typically large-scale, equipped not only with basic hot spring pools but also spas, fine-dining restaurants, KTV, and game rooms. This "hot spring resort" model turns soaking from individual healing into a multi-person social gathering activity. You can book a private bathhouse for family time, chat and laugh with friends in communal pools, or even sing KTV in private rooms by the hot spring.

**Private bathhouse culture is a Taiwanese innovation.** In contrast to the Japanese tradition of communal pools, Taiwan has developed refined private spaces, from simple indoor bathhouses to luxurious open-air rotenburo, priced from NT$800 to NT$3,000. This design caters to Taiwanese emphasis on privacy while creating new options for couples' dates and family gatherings.

Beitou's Tangsen Hot Spring Hall perfectly exemplifies Taiwan-style hot spring characteristics. Its pools are categorized by function: 38°C beauty skin pool, 42°C stress-relief massage pool, and the "ice-and-fire five-layer heaven" experience of alternating hot and cold. Visitors can freely choose according to their needs — this customized experience is the essence of Taiwan-style hot springs.

But Taiwan-style hot springs also retain the core value of healing. Many hot spring areas offer professional spa treatments combining aromatherapy, massage, and beauty care, creating "hot spring+" composite experiences.

> **📝 Curator's Perspective** Taiwan's hot springs reflect the traits of an island society — valuing relational connection over individual cultivation, preferring liveliness over solitude, pragmatism over formality.

## The Jiaoxi Phenomenon: The Urban Miracle of a Plain Hot Spring

Jiaoxi represents another innovation in Taiwan's hot spring culture: the "hot spring city."

Unlike most hot springs located in mountainous areas, Jiaoxi sits on the Lanyang Plain, with hot springs welling up throughout the entire urban area. This rare geological condition has let Jiaoxi develop the concept of "urban hot springs" — enjoying premium hot springs without leaving convenient urban amenities.

**Jiaoxi's sodium bicarbonate springs are acclaimed as "beauty springs."** The colorless, odorless water (pH 7–8), rich in sodium, potassium, and calcium ions, is gentle and non-irritating to skin, leaving it smooth after soaking — particularly popular with female visitors. A 2024 Online Temperature Gauge survey found Jiaoxi winning "Taiwan's Most Popular Hot Spring Area" champion for three consecutive years, surpassing the longer-established Beitou.

Tangweigou Hot Spring Park showcases Jiaoxi's democratic spirit. This free-access hot spring park uses bamboo screens for privacy and LED lighting for romantic atmosphere, letting everyone experience hot spring culture without barriers. Weekends at Tangweigou are always bustling: seniors soaking foot baths while chatting, couples experiencing fish therapy in hot spring fish pools, families cooking hot spring eggs together at the egg-boiling area.

Since the opening of the Hsuehshan Tunnel, the drive from Taipei to Jiaoxi takes only one hour, creating a new "commuter-style soaking" model. Weekend streets in Jiaoxi are filled with Taipei license plates; hot spring inns are fully booked, and many Taipei residents treat Jiaoxi as their "hot spring living room."

**Jiaoxi's success lies in its accessibility.** Options span every price point: NT$100 public foot baths, NT$150 fish therapy, NT$600 communal bathhouses, NT$2,000 private rotenburo, NT$8,000 luxury hot spring suites. Whatever the budget, a suitable hot spring experience can be found.

Yilan County Government statistics show Jiaoxi Hot Spring Area received 3.5 million visitors in 2024, with hot spring-related industries generating annual revenue exceeding NT$4.5 billion — Taiwan's highest economic benefit for a single hot spring area.

## Hot Spring Cuisine: Creative Experiments in Geothermal Cooking

Taiwan's hot spring culture has also developed unique "geothermal cuisine" innovations.

**Hot spring eggs are the classic representative of geothermal cooking.** Using 60–70°C hot spring water for long-duration heating lets the egg white set while the yolk remains semi-soft. This cooking method's distinctive texture, paired with Japanese soy sauce and shichimi togarashi, has become a must-try affordable delicacy in hot spring areas. At the geothermal cooking zones of Beitou Thermal Valley and Jiaoxi's Tangweigou, visitors can personally cook hot spring eggs, corn, and sweet potatoes, experiencing the joy of an "earth kitchen."

**Guanziling's barrel chicken (桶仔雞) is a hot spring cuisine classic.** Free-range chicken slow-roasted using natural geothermal heat yields crispy skin and tender meat, with a subtle mineral aroma from the unique cooking method. Paired with Guanziling specialties mountain pepper (山胡椒) and maqaw (破布子), it creates an irreplicable local flavor.

Innovative operators have even attempted "hot spring ramen" — using hot spring water for the broth, claimed to have a special sweetness. Though scientifically difficult to prove hot spring water's actual impact on taste, this conceptual combination creates buzz, becoming a hot spring area specialty.

**Hot spring agriculture is another interesting experiment.** Guanziling uses geothermal heat to grow tomatoes; Beitou uses hot spring water to cultivate water spinach (kangkong). Though results are debated, these attempts showcase the innovative spirit of Taiwan's hot spring industry and open new possibilities for agriculture.

> **🍽️ Food Culture Perspective** Hot spring cuisine is not merely appetite satisfaction, but concrete practice of human-land connection. Cooking food with earth's thermal energy is the most ancient yet most modern cooking method.

## Smart Hot Springs in the Digital Age

Technology is redefining Taiwan's hot spring experience.

**Smart water quality monitoring systems** make soaking more reassuring. Upscale hot spring resorts in Beitou, Taipei have installed real-time monitoring equipment, letting visitors check current spring water pH, mineral content, temperature, and other data via screens. This transparent service boosts consumer confidence and drives industry-wide quality improvement.

**Online reservation systems** solve peak-season supply shortages. Through apps, visitors can pre-book bathhouses, select preferred time slots and room types, avoiding on-site waits. During the pandemic, this contactless service proved crucial, helping many operators survive.

**Themed hot springs** attract younger generations. Creative combinations like petal baths, milk baths, red wine baths, and coffee baths — though departing from tradition — generate buzz in the social media era. These Instagram-friendly designs draw young visitors, injecting new vitality into the traditional hot spring industry.

**VR hot spring experiences** are the next frontier. Some operators are trialing virtual reality in hot springs, letting visitors tour world landscapes or experience guided meditation while soaking.

Smart hot springs aim not to replace traditional experience, but to lower barriers, enhance quality, and expand clientele. The core remains physical and mental healing; technology is merely a better vehicle.

## Sustainability Challenges: Protecting Earth's Gifts

Taiwan's hot spring industry faces critical sustainability challenges.

**Overdevelopment is the greatest hidden worry.** Large-scale construction in popular hot spring areas may affect natural spring outflow and water quality. Lushan Hot Spring in Nantou was fully banned from construction due to geological disasters — a severe warning against overdevelopment. Environmental groups estimate approximately 30% of Taiwan's hot spring areas face overdevelopment risk.

**Climate change** brings new threats. Extreme rainfall may affect spring water quality; long-term droughts may reduce volume. Some hot spring areas already show declining water temperatures and reduced outflow, requiring more precise monitoring and protection measures.

The current Hot Spring Act establishes basic development controls, but faces emerging formats (urban hot springs, hot spring B&Bs, smart hot springs, etc.) needing more refined regulations. Especially in Indigenous areas, hot spring development must balance economic growth with cultural preservation.

Japan, South Korea, and Iceland are actively promoting hot spring tourism. Taiwan must enhance international competitiveness while maintaining quality. Developing differentiated paths — ecological hot springs, cultural hot springs, health hot springs — may be the breakthrough.

> **⚠️ Sustainability Mindset** Hot springs are Earth's gift to Taiwan. We must learn to cherish and use them sustainably, not squander and consume. The next generation also has the right to enjoy this warmth from the planet's heart.

## Hot Spring Etiquette: Balancing Modern and Traditional

Basic etiquette remains important when experiencing Taiwan's hot spring culture.

Showering before entering pools is basic respect for water quality maintenance and other visitors. Use soap to thoroughly cleanse the body, ensuring personal hygiene. Control soaking time — 10–15 minutes per session with rest breaks is recommended, avoiding over-soaking that causes physical discomfort. If feeling dizzy, heart palpitations, or unwell, immediately leave the pool for a cool, shaded area.

Maintain quiet, avoiding loud noise that disturbs others. Hot springs are healing spaces; quiet environments aid physical and mental relaxation. Set phones to silent mode; avoid phone calls poolside.

Respect facility rules, including whether swimwear is required, whether towels are permitted, etc. Private bathhouses have time limits; please return them promptly so the next guests can use them smoothly.

Taiwan's hot spring culture, from accidental military medical needs a century ago, has evolved into today's diverse life experience combining healing, leisure, and socializing. In this process, we not only preserved the refinement of Japanese hot springs but also infused Taiwanese creativity and human warmth, creating the world's unique "Taiwan-style hot spring civilization."

The next time you soak in a hot spring, what you feel is not just geothermal warmth, but a healing movement co-written by a century of cultural fusion, geological gifts, and humanistic innovation — this is the true value of Taiwan's hot spring culture.
