# Taiwanese Agriculture and Rural Regeneration

In the green onion fields of Sanxing Township, Yilan, a 33-year-old youth who returned to his hometown is using his smartphone to livestream the harvesting process, introducing the cultivation secrets of organic Sanxing scallions to online viewers. On the other side of the screen, consumers in Taipei have already placed pre-orders. This scene would have been unimaginable 50 years ago—at that time, farmers were worrying about rice overproduction, and young people were migrating en masse to factories for work. Today, Taiwan's agriculture has transformed from a "mass-produced rice kingdom" into a "paradigm of precision agriculture," and rural areas have evolved from declining corners losing population into new frontiers where creativity and sustainability coexist.

> Cultivated land in Taiwan accounts for about 20% of the national territory. In early 2024, primary agriculture, forestry, fishery, and livestock sectors accounted for only 1.5% of the national GDP; however, when including food and beverage manufacturing, wholesale, retail, and catering, the gross output of the agricultural and agri-food chain reached NT$1.7449 trillion, accounting for 6.8% of GDP. The number of employed persons is approximately 2 million, representing 17.2% of the national total, with 490,000 people (4.3%) [^1] in the primary agriculture, forestry, fishery, and livestock sectors.

## Why This Matters

Agriculture is the foundation of Taiwan's culture and ecology, as well as a vital industry supporting society as a whole. From rice breeding during the Japanese colonial period and post-war land reform to today's precision agriculture, the history of Taiwan's agricultural development is a history of social transformation. As the world faces food security challenges, Taiwan's experience in agricultural transformation—how to achieve high value on limited land and how to revitalize rural areas through community building—provides a valuable reference for many developing nations.

## From Rice Kingdom to Precision Agriculture

### 1950–1980: The Golden Age of the Rice Kingdom

**Land Reform as a Foundation:**
The land reforms between 1949 and 1953 replaced the large-landlord system of the Japanese colonial era with the principle of "land to the tiller," stimulating farmers' productivity. Coupled with the promotion of superior _Penglai_ (蓬萊) rice varieties, Taiwan's rice production increased rapidly.

**The Green Revolution:**

- Introduction of International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) technologies in the 1960s.
- Promotion of chemical fertilizers and pesticides.
- Establishment of irrigation and water conservancy systems.
- Significant increase in rice yield per unit area over the three decades following the war; from the 1970s onward, Taiwan faced rice overproduction [^3].

**Establishment of the Farmers' Association System:**
Farmers' Associations became a bridge between farmers and the government, responsible for:

- Promotion of agricultural technology.
- Procurement and marketing of agricultural products.
- Agricultural financial services.
- Farmer education and training.

### 1980–2000: Industrial Restructuring

As industrialization accelerated, agriculture faced labor outflow and declining comparative advantages:

**The Rice Surplus Crisis:**

- In the 1980s, due to rice overproduction, the government promoted "rice reduction policies."
- Encouraged switching to high-economic-value crops.
- Began focusing on agricultural product quality rather than quantity.

**Diversified Development:**

- Improved cultivation techniques for vegetables and fruits.
- Rise of the floriculture and horticulture industry.
- Modernization of the livestock industry.
- Breakthroughs in aquaculture technology.

### 2000–Present: The Precision Agriculture Paradigm

**Precision Agriculture Policy:**
Since the 1980s, agricultural authorities have used "precision agriculture" as a direction for transformation. In 2009, the Executive Yuan passed the "Healthy and Excellent Precision Agriculture Program," designating it as one of the six emerging industries [^2], emphasizing:

- High quality and high added value.
- Environmentally friendly and sustainable development.
- Application of technology and innovation.
- Branding and internationalization.

## The Path to Rural Community Regeneration

### Rural Community Building Movement

**1990s Enlightenment in Community Building:**
In 1994, the Council for Cultural Affairs proposed "Overall Community Building," drawing on Japanese town-making and the "One Village, One Product" experiences. Taiwan began promoting community building:

- Discovering local characteristics and cultural resources.
- Cultivating community awareness and identity.
- Integrating industrial development with tourism.
- Establishing community self-management capabilities.

**Success Stories:**

**Xinpu, Hsinchu:**
By combining Hakka culture with the century-old dried persimmon industry—relying on the _Jiuxiang_ (九降) wind to dry the fruit—it has developed cultural tourism [^8].

- Preserving traditional dried persimmon production techniques.
- Renovating historical sites and buildings.
- Developing persimmon festivals and activities.
- Establishing brands and distribution channels for dried persimmons.

**Taomi Community, Nantou:**
A model of reconstruction after the 921 earthquake, transforming from ruins into an ecological village.

- Rebuilding homes using ecological construction methods.
- Developing ecotourism and environmental education.
- The Paper Dome became a symbol of community rebirth.
- Serving as a base for amphibian ecological research and conservation.

### Rural Regeneration Act and Practice

**Passage of the Rural Regeneration Act in 2010:**

- Article 7 of the _Rural Regeneration Act_ (promulgated in 2010) stipulates the establishment of a Rural Regeneration Fund of NT$150 billion, to be allocated annually over ten years [^5].
- Promoting regeneration projects at the community level.
- Combining hardware construction with software improvement.
- Emphasizing bottom-up participatory planning, with communities receiving guidance on proposals from the Agency of Rural Development and Water Conservation [^6].

**Regeneration Strategies:**

1. **Industrial Vitalization:** Developing specialty agricultural products and the "six-level" integration of agriculture.
2. **Cultural Preservation:** Maintaining traditional architecture and intangible cultural heritage.
3. **Environmental Improvement:** Renovating irrigation channels, trails, and public spaces.
4. **Talent Cultivation:** Encouraging youth to return to rural areas and training community leaders.

## The Wave of Youth Returning to Farming

### The Rise of "New Farmers"

**The Return-to-Farm Trend after 2010:**
Influenced by food safety concerns and the pursuit of quality of life, more young people are choosing to return home to farm. Most of these "new farmers" bring non-agricultural professional backgrounds, prioritizing environmentally friendly practices and brand management. The Ministry of Agriculture supports them through programs such as the "Top 100 Young Farmers."

### New Generation Agricultural Business Models

**Smart Agriculture:**

- Smart greenhouses and environmental control systems.
- Drone spraying and monitoring.
- IoT (Internet of Things) sensor applications.
- Big data analysis and AI-assisted decision-making.

**Branded Management:**

- Establishing personal or farm brands.
- Food and agriculture education and experiential activities.
- Social media marketing.
- Direct-to-consumer sales (farm-to-table/direct delivery).

**Six-Level Industrialization:**

- Primary Production: Safe, high-quality agricultural products.
- Secondary Processing: Refinement and packaging of agricultural products.
- Tertiary Services: Tourism experiences and catering services.
- The integrated benefit of 1 × 2 × 3 = 6.

### Successful Returnee Cases

**Dapi Pickled Cabbage, Yunlin:**
Dapi Township, the top producer of pickled cabbage in Taiwan, has rebranded a traditional industry once notorious for pickling wastewater into a local brand [^9]:

- In 2003, a specialized pickled cabbage zone was established to centralize wastewater treatment.
- Collaborating with National Taiwan University's College of Life Science to convert thousands of tons of high-salt wastewater annually into animal feed and liquid fertilizer.
- Using pickled cabbage restaurants and straw crafts to turn the industry into an entry point for food education and tourism.

**Lina-li Tribe, Pingtung:**
Following the 2009 Morakot typhoon, three tribes—Dashé Village in Sandimen, Majia Village in Majia, and Haocha Village in Wutai—relocated to homes rebuilt at the Majia Farm [^10]:

- Traditional crops like red quinoa, millet, and pigeon pea have returned to tribal tables and products.
- The Majia Granary (_Kubav_) turns red quinoa into workshops and flavored meals.
- Tribal ecotourism runs alongside indigenous cultural education.

## The Rise of Organic Agriculture

### Organic Development History

The development of organic agriculture in Taiwan can be divided into three periods. During the germination period from the late 1980s to the 1990s, the _Zhu Fu_ (主婦) Alliance (founded in 1987) promoted group buying, and the Tzu Chi Organic Agriculture Development Foundation was established in 1997 as consumer environmental awareness began to awaken [^11]. In 2007, the _Agricultural Products Production and Verification Management Act_ established a third-party verification system; at that time, organic certified area accounted for only 0.24% of cultivated land. Since the enactment of the _Organic Agriculture Promotion Act_ in 2018, the industry has entered an acceleration phase: by the end of 2025, organic certified area reached 21,871 hectares (2.81% of cultivated land), and by August 2026, there were 6,600 organic certified households covering 23,631 hectares [^7][^12].

### Challenges and Opportunities in Organic Agriculture

The main challenges for organic agriculture lie in income loss during the transition period, high thresholds for organic pest control technology, and difficulty in establishing sales channels; consumer price sensitivity remains a constraining factor. On the other hand, rising food safety awareness is driving demand, which, combined with government subsidies, the growth of international organic markets, and integration with ecotourism, has significantly strengthened the growth momentum of organic agriculture.

## Evolution of the Farmers' Association System

### Traditional Functions of Farmers' Associations

Traditional Farmers' Associations served dual political and economic functions: politically, they represented farmers' interests, promoted agricultural policies, and mobilized grassroots rural organizations; economically, they executed collective marketing of agricultural products, joint procurement of agricultural materials, agricultural financial and insurance services, and the promotion of agricultural technology.

### Modern Transformation of Farmers' Associations

Modern Farmers' Association services have extended from agriculture to supermarket retail, leisure tourism and catering, long-term care community support, and cultural education. Simultaneously, they are advancing digital transformation—online shopping platforms, digital payments, agricultural big data analysis, and smart agricultural services—forming four dimensions of digital upgrading for the associations.

**Successful Transformation Case:**

**Gukeng Farmers' Association, Yunlin:** Centered on the establishment of the Gukeng Coffee brand, it has extended into coffee culture festivals, coffee-themed tourism, and premium agricultural product management, forming a complete path from industry to culture.

## Agricultural Technological Innovation

### Smart Agriculture Development

Facility agriculture focuses on smart greenhouses, automated irrigation and fertilization, LED plant factories, and vertical farming technologies. Precision agriculture integrates GPS-guided farm machinery, variable-rate fertilization, crop growth monitoring, and yield prediction models. The application of biotechnology includes breeding disease-resistant varieties, developing microbial fertilizers, gene-marker-assisted selection, and tissue culture seedling production; these three dimensions constitute the technical framework of Taiwan's smart agriculture.

### Digital Agriculture Platforms

The Traceability System uses QR codes to trace the production process, presenting food safety information directly to consumers to build trust and enhance brand value. Agricultural e-commerce platforms integrate online sales, direct delivery, farm experience bookings, and agricultural knowledge sharing, allowing farmers to reach consumers directly by bypassing middlemen.

## Future Challenges

### Climate Change Adaptation

Policy tools for climate change adaptation include breeding drought-resistant and flood-tolerant varieties, strengthening disaster prevention in facility agriculture, improving agricultural insurance systems, and establishing post-disaster replanting guidance mechanisms. Sustainable production models are being advanced across four dimensions: carbon-sink agriculture, circular agriculture, biodiversity conservation, and soil health maintenance.

### Food Security Assurance

In the face of international food price volatility and supply chain risks, Taiwan's food security strategy includes increasing self-sufficiency rates, establishing strategic grain reserves, developing alternative protein sources, and strengthening regional food cooperation.

### Generational Succession and Innovation

The focus of agricultural technology succession lies in preserving traditional farming knowledge, facilitating experience exchange between generations, improving the agricultural education system, and cultivating professional farmers; industrial upgrading is directed toward high-value agriculture, the rise of agricultural services, international market expansion, and brand value construction.

## New Hope for Rural Areas

Today's Taiwanese rural areas are no longer the backward regions of traditional perception. From the persistence of small organic farmers and the creativity of returning youth to the transformation of the Farmers' Association system, rural communities are redefining their value. This land carries multiple energies: food production, cultural inheritance, ecological conservation, and innovation testing.

Under the dual challenges of globalization and climate change, Taiwan's experience in agricultural and rural transformation may serve as an important reference for the world seeking a path toward sustainable development. Having undergone three stages of transformation—from a rice kingdom to precision agriculture and then to rural community regeneration—Taiwan's path to creating high value on limited land is a rare operational template for smallholder economies.
