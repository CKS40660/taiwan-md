# EEWH Green Building Label: How a Subtropical Island Wrote Climate into Building Code

> **30-second overview:** Taiwan's EEWH green building assessment system launched in 1999 as the world's fourth and Asia's first system of its kind. It grew from seven to nine indicators, added five-tier grading (Diamond, Gold, Silver, Bronze, Qualified) in 2004, split into five specialized manuals in 2011, and introduced building energy performance labeling in 2021. Its design philosophy is "affordability": rather than encouraging expensive equipment, it incorporates ventilation, shading, and water retention—the fundamentals of subtropical climate design—into the scoring system.

---

## One Building, Nine Checkpoints

EEWH stands for four English words: Ecology, Energy Saving, Waste Reduction, and Health. These four principles are broken down into nine indicators: biodiversity, greenery, site water retention, daily energy conservation, CO₂ reduction, waste reduction, indoor environment, water resources, and wastewater and waste management.[^1]

To earn the label, a building must score points across these indicators, with the final grade determined by total score. After 2011, different building types use different manuals: general new or existing buildings use the Basic Category (EEWH-BC), residential uses EEWH-RS, industrial facilities use EEWH-GF, retrofitted buildings use EEWH-RN, and communities use EEWH-EC.[^2]

## Not the World's Second, but Fourth

Taiwan often claims EEWH is the "world's second" national-level green building label. This claim does not appear in official documents.

The Architecture and Building Research Institute's 2012 manual states clearly: the world's first green building assessment system was Britain's BREEAM (1990), which influenced the United States' LEED (1996) and Canada's GBTool (1998); "Taiwan's green building assessment system EEWH, established in 1999, is a dark horse from Asia and the world's fourth system to launch." Japan's CASBEE and Australia's Energy Star did not begin until 2002.[^2]

The correct positioning is: fourth globally, first in Asia, and the world's first system "developed independently based on subtropical building energy-efficiency characteristics," without adopting European or American frameworks.[^2] This ranking may not sound as impressive as "second," but the technical significance is more noteworthy: Taiwan did not transplant temperate-zone standards but redesigned the assessment methodology for its own climate.

## From Seven to Nine Indicators

In 1995, the Executive Yuan's Global Change Policy Steering Group and the Architecture and Building Research Institute began studying Taiwan's subtropical climate and domestic building characteristics.[^1] In 1999, the green building label system officially launched with seven indicators: greenery, site water retention, water resources, daily energy conservation, CO₂ reduction, waste reduction, and wastewater and waste management.[^1]

In 2003, the system added "biodiversity" and "indoor environment," creating today's nine indicators.[^1] This addition brought ecology and human health into the scoring framework, transforming "green building" from a pure energy-efficiency competition.

## The 2001 Policy Lever

Whether a system spreads depends on procurement. In 2001, the Executive Yuan approved the "Green Building Promotion Plan"; in 2003, it launched a four-year initiative requiring all publicly funded new buildings with construction budgets of NT$50 million or above to obtain a "Candidate Green Building Certificate."[^3]

This threshold transformed green building from voluntary application into a mandatory condition for public works. The impact is directly reflected in numbers: by the end of 2011, nearly 3,000 certified green building labels and candidate certificates had been awarded.[^3] Subsequently, the Executive Yuan launched the "Ecological City Green Building Promotion Plan" in 2008 and the "Smart Green Building Promotion Plan" in 2010, expanding the scope from individual buildings to cities and the ICT industry.[^3]

## Grading, and the Birth of a Family

In 2004, the system introduced five-tier grading (Diamond, Gold, Silver, Bronze, Qualified) and established the "Green Building Material Label" certification.[^3] Wikipedia records the grading system's establishment as 2007, which conflicts with the official manual's 2004 date; this article follows the official record.[^1][^3]

In 2011, five specialized manuals were completed, and EEWH transitioned from "one-size-fits-all" to "category-specific assessment."[^2] The industrial facility category (EEWH-GF) is particularly distinctive: it requires senior management to issue a commitment letter and introduces a "Testing, Adjusting, and Balancing" (TAB) performance verification system for central air-conditioning facilities of 500RT or larger, incorporating equipment acceptance into the scoring framework.[^2]

## The Weight of Numbers

In November 2021, certified green building labels and candidate certificates nationwide exceeded 10,000. The manual's preface notes that green building's "ecology, energy conservation, waste reduction, and health" have become common language among government, academia, and media, even appearing in middle and elementary school textbooks, with local governments establishing green building ordinances.[^3]

For an assessment system to reach this scale relies not on technological leadership but on deliberately low thresholds and costs. The manual contains a remarkably candid self-description: EEWH insists on composite performance scoring rather than "menu-based" assessment with independent point allocation for each item, allowing designers to weigh priorities and choose economical technology combinations, avoiding over-equipment and excess investment; assessment items focus only on basic environmental benefits directly related to architecture and urban planning, excluding content from other sectors like transportation and environmental protection, and do not encourage expensive green procurement.[^2]

The trade-off is that thresholds appear more lenient, with simpler operations and shorter certification timelines. The benefit is universalizability: when a system serves only rare demonstration projects, it proves the technical ceiling; when it must cover ninety percent of the building market, it must first address cost and process.

## Net-Zero Building: The Next Step

In 2021, the Architecture and Building Research Institute introduced building energy performance assessment and labeling (EEWH-BERS) into the green building system, referencing the European Union's Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) and defining "near-zero carbon buildings" (NZCB) as residential with 40% or greater carbon reduction and non-residential with 50% or greater energy savings, then applying the EU EN 15217 seven-stage labeling system, with "1+" as the highest grade, descending to grades 1 through 7.[^3]

The manual explains this step clearly: the net-zero building mandate falls to the Ministry of the Interior, and building energy performance labeling is "the internationally recognized most precise and most effective national building energy management tool," capable of raising Taiwan's green building regulatory capacity for building energy from seventy percent to over ninety percent.[^3]

## How Far Can One System Go

EEWH's story is not technological breakthrough but the process of institutionalizing climate conditions: typhoons, humid heat, western sun exposure, water scarcity—the conditions Taiwan's buildings face daily—have been written into measurable, scorable, certifiable indicators.

More than two decades later, its problems are as clear as its strengths. Lenient thresholds enable widespread certification but also create a gap between "earning the label" and "actually saving electricity"; grading systems let consumers distinguish quality but also turn Diamond grade into marketing language. The next step—building energy performance labeling—is partly designed to close this gap: moving from "claiming to be green at the design stage" to "proving how much electricity you use during operation."

Whether this path succeeds depends not on how complete the manual is, but on how many buildings are willing to produce their electricity bills.
