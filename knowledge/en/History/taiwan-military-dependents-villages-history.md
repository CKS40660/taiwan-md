# History of Taiwan's Military Dependents' Villages

> **30-Second Overview:** Starting in the late 1940s, the Nationalist government built military dependents' villages (_juancun_) to settle families who accompanied the military to Taiwan. By 1984, there were 888 such villages under the management of the Ministry of National Defense; 1982 statistics show approximately 470,000 people living in these settlements. One such example is Zhongzheng New Village in Taoyuan, built in the autumn of 1954 for the families of the Yunnan Anti-Communist Salvation Army who retreated from northern Myanmar. From bamboo fences to modern national housing, these villages eventually evolved into an important genetic component of Taiwan's diverse culture.

In the autumn of 1954, in Longgang at the junction of Pingzhen and Zhongli in Taoyuan, the Ministry of National Defense constructed approximately 530 rudimentary houses (Wikipedia records 534; some sources say 503) [^1]. Built from bamboo fences, yellow mud, and corrugated iron, these homes averaged less than 33 square meters (10 _ping_) per household, yet they carried the fates of a unique group of residents—not families who arrived directly with the Nationalist government, but the dependents of the "Lost Army" (_gujun_) who traveled from mainland China to Burma and then to Taiwan.

**The birth of Zhongzheng New Village illustrates the complex character of _juancun_ culture: it is not merely a dialogue between Mainlanders and local Taiwanese, but a dialectic of exile and settlement, the temporary and the permanent, and home versus a foreign land.**

## The Complex Spectrum of a Great Migration

### 1.2 Million People, Multiple Paths

While "1.2 million military and civilian personnel followed the government to Taiwan" is a common saying, the reality was far more complex. These groups arrived in several waves between 1945 and 1954:

- **Around 1949**: Approximately 600,000 troops retreated to Taiwan, along with civil servants and civilians [^2].
- **1950**: Following the retreat from the Zhoushan Archipelago, over 120,000 soldiers and approximately 20,000 local residents were evacuated to Taiwan within three days [^3].
- **1953**: The Huang Jie troops stationed on Phu Quoc Island, Vietnam, were transported back to Taiwan, totaling about 30,000 people [^4].
- **Late 1953 to 1954**: The Yunnan Anti-Communist Salvation Army retreated from northern Myanmar in three batches; the first retreat consisted of 6,986 people [^5]. Additionally, over 14,000 prisoners of war from the Chinese People's Volunteers during the Korean War chose to come to Taiwan, with the first batch arriving in Keelung on January 23, 1954 [^6].

**This was not a single retreat, but nearly a decade of intermittent exile.** Behind every wave of migration lay different forms of despair and hope. While many military families who arrived directly were settled by the government, the dependents of the "Lost Army" experienced a double exile—first the loss of their homeland, then a struggle for survival on the borderlands.

### The Unique Fate of the Lost Army

The Yunnan Anti-Communist Salvation Army, commanded by Li Mi, experienced one of the most circuitous journeys among all those who moved to Taiwan. After the Nationalist forces were defeated in Yunnan in late 1949, this unit did not retreat directly to Taiwan but instead withdrew into northern Myanmar, fighting guerrilla warfare along the China-Burma border for nearly four years.

> **📝 Curator's Note**  
> The "lost" (_gu_) in "Lost Army" refers not only to geographical isolation but also to political ambiguity. They were neither recognized by the Burmese government nor accepted by the international community, and even their status as "allied forces" remained murky.

After the Burmese government complained to the United Nations regarding the occupation of its territory by Nationalist troops, the Republic of China (ROC) government consulted with the US, Thailand, and Burma and decided to withdraw the troops from Burma. Starting in late 1953, Li Mi led the 193rd Division and their families in batches back to Taiwan [^1]. Instead of a heroic welcome, these men, women, and children were met with the reality of being dispersed into temporary quarters in warehouses belonging to the Taiwan Sugar Corporation in places like Xizhou, Dalin, and Wufeng. The scarcity of supplies was "even more severe than during the guerrilla warfare in northern Myanmar" [^7], which led to the Ministry of National Defense's decision to build Zhongzheng New Village in Taoyuan in the autumn of 1954.

Zhongzheng New Village is often called "the first military dependents' village built after the Nationalist government moved to Taiwan" [^7], but this claim does not hold up. Si Si Nan Cun in Sanzhangli, Taipei, was built in 1948 [^8]; Huangpu New Village in Fengshan was occupied by Sun Li-jen's troops in 1949 [^9]; and Qiaoai New Village in Daxi, Taoyuan, was also among the first batches of villages constructed [^10]. **Zhongzheng New Village was simply one of the villages built by the Ministry of National Defense for the late-arriving Lost Army.** Its uniqueness lies not in its chronology, but in the origins of its residents: the accents and cuisines of Yunnan, Thailand, and Burma made it distinct from other _juancun_ from the very beginning.

## Establishing the Bamboo Fence Republic

### Military Geography

The spatial distribution of these villages reflected military branch politics. Many villages were divided by service branch or specific unit; in many cases, almost all males in a single village belonged to the same branch or even the same unit [^7]:

- **Zuoying, Kaohsiung**: Navy villages; many Shandong natives gathered here, and the markets primarily featured wheat-based noodles [^11].
- **Qingquangang, Taichung**: Air Force villages.
- **Zhongzheng New Village, Taoyuan**: Yunnan-Burma guerrilla forces; strong presence of Yunnan Baiyi culture.
- **Erkong, Rende, Tainan**: Air Force; primarily people from Sichuan and Hunan [^11].

> **💡 Did You Know?**  
> Based on modern Chinese administrative divisions, the largest number of soldiers who came to Taiwan were from Shandong Province (72,604), followed by Guangdong (66,613) and Jiangsu (54,950) [^2]. These numbers directly determined the "dialect maps" of the villages.

This pattern of military clustering served both administrative needs and as a natural extension of military culture. The bonds formed on the battlefield became the neighborhood support systems in Taiwan. **The _juancun_ was not just a residential area; it was a civilian extension of military culture.**

### Material Hardship, Spiritual Abundance

The material conditions of early villages were extremely primitive. In Zhongzheng New Village, houses "averaged less than 10 _ping_, with only about 4.5 to 5 _ping_ actually providing shelter from the elements" [^7]. Living rooms doubled as bedrooms, and kitchens could often only fit a single stove. With bamboo fences, mud-filled cracks, and corrugated iron roofs, lightning storms posed a risk of electric shock.

Yet, amidst this scarcity, the residents developed incredible creativity:

- Using empty gasoline cans as water wells.
- Using scrap wood to create room dividers.
- Using broken umbrellas as sunshades.
- Repurposing flour sacks into bedsheets and clothing.

**"Hardship philosophy" was not a choice; it was a survival skill.** However, it was precisely this shared experience of struggle that fostered the unique community cohesion characteristic of _juancun_.

## Reconstructing Culinary Memories

### Folk Fusion of the Eight Major Cuisines

The most tangible cultural heritage of the villages is their food. Mothers from all over China, working with limited ingredients and makeshift kitchens, reconstructed the "Eight Major Cuisines" of China—but this reconstruction was actually a process of innovation.

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**  
> Writer Jiao Tong once said: "Sichuan has no Sichuan-style beef noodles; Mongolia has no Mongolian barbecue; Fuzhou has no Fuzhou noodles." [^11] Is _juancun_ cuisine "original hometown flavor" or "Taiwanese flavor"? The answer may be both, or neither.

**Three characteristics of _juancun_ cuisine:**

1. **Localization through local ingredients**: Using Taiwanese vegetables to make hometown dishes and adjusting seasonings to suit local palates.
2. **Economical pragmatism**: One pot of stewed food to feed a whole family; one piece of tofu transformed into ten different dishes.
3. **Cross-provincial technical exchange**: Shandong mothers learning Sichuan spice techniques, or Jiangnan mothers adopting Cantonese stir-fry methods.

Beef noodles are the best example. After the government moved to Taiwan, Sichuan-style beef noodles took root in Kaohsiung; later, stalls appeared around Yongkang Street and Xinyi Road in Taipei. In central and southern Taiwan, braised beef noodles were often prepared using Chinese medicinal herbs instead of _doubanjiang_ (broad bean paste) [^11]. **"Beef noodles" became a representative Taiwanese snack, yet "Sichuan-style beef noodles" do not exist in Sichuan.**

### Markets as Cultural Intersections

The village markets were the front lines of cultural exchange between Mainlanders and local Taiwanese. Taking Zhongzheng Market as an example, it began with vegetable farmers from nearby Xiaoli pushing carts into the villages to sell goods [^7]. As village mothers became regular customers, a marketplace gradually formed.

**This seemingly simple commercial relationship was actually the first deep contact between two communities.** Local Taiwanese learned Mainlander tastes, while Mainlanders became familiar with Taiwanese ingredients. _Shaobing_ (baked flatbread) and _youtiao_ (fried dough sticks), _lu rou fan_ (braised pork rice), soy milk, and traditional black tea began to coexist in the same market.

## Three Generations of Identity Shifts

### The First Generation: Eternal Nostalgia

The first generation of residents maintained a constant longing for their homelands. For them, "China" was not a political entity, but the memory of Shandong steamed buns, Sichuan braised pork, or Hunan cured meats and sausages.

Because the government initially expected a swift "counterattack to the mainland," village designs were intended to be temporary. The rudimentary housing and lack of infrastructure reflected an optimistic expectation that they would "return home soon." **But three years turned into thirty; the temporary became permanent, and the villages became reluctant "little hometowns."**

Language policy also reflected this mindset. While _juancun_ strongly promoted Mandarin education, this "Mandarin" carried more than just communicative function—it was a vessel for cultural identity. Children in these villages could recite the names of rivers in the Yangtze basin but might not know the name of the stream next door.

### The Second Generation: Identity Tug-of-War

The second generation faced unprecedented identity dilemmas. They grew up in the villages with an education rooted in Chinese culture, but the land they lived on was Taiwan. After the lifting of martial law in 1987 and the opening of family visits, this division became more intense.

**An interesting paradox emerged:** When the first-generation veterans finally returned to their hometowns for visits, many found themselves "unable to adapt." Forty years of separation had turned their "hometown" into a foreign land. Conversely, while Taiwan was once a "temporary residence," it had become their true home.

This led to diverging political stances among the second generation: some supported unification, some supported independence, and many more supported maintaining the status quo. **"I am a child of the _juancun_, and I am also Taiwanese"—this is not a contradiction, but a reality.**

### The Third Generation: Cultural Root-Seeking

Most of the third generation did not grow up in the villages; their memories are primarily derived from their parents' stories. Paradoxically, it is this generation that has spearheaded the "rescue" movement for _juancun_ culture.

Through fieldwork, oral histories, and digital archiving, the third generation is rediscovering these villages. This "cultural root-seeking" reflects a desire for cultural origins in a globalized era. **They are not searching for political identity, but for the richness of cultural identity.**

## The Tug-of-War Between Demolition and Preservation

### 1996: The Double-Edged Sword of the Reconstruction Act

The promulgation of the _Act for the Reconstruction of Old Military Dependents' Villages of the Armed Forces_ in February 1996 [^12] marked the beginning of the large-scale disappearance of these villages. The policy goals were practical: improve living conditions, release prime land for development, and meet urban growth needs.

> **📊 Data Source**  
> In 1984, there were 888 villages under the management of the Ministry of National Defense, comprising 109,786 households. Following the implementation of the Reconstruction Act, these numbers decreased annually; by 2001, only 530 public villages remained under management [^10]. In 2018, during the opening of the Kaohsiung Military Dependents' Village Cultural Park, Secretary-General of the Presidential Office Chen Chu stated there were 886 such villages across Taiwan [^13].

While reconstructed villages became modern housing and solved living quality issues, the original settlement patterns disappeared, and neighborhood ties weakened. **Those who moved into new apartments enjoyed modern conveniences but lost the sense of community where "one family's barbecue makes the whole neighborhood hungry."**

The reconstruction process was not smooth. Disputes over allocation, relocation compensation, and emotional loss sparked many protests. A deeper question remained: **When a _juancun_ becomes modern housing, can "village culture" survive?**

### Awakening of Preservation: From Illegal Structures to Cultural Heritage

Baozangyan (Treasure Rock) is a key case in the preservation movement. This hillside settlement near Gongguan in Taipei was originally classified as "illegal structures" facing total demolition. However, through local advocacy, it was registered as a "Historical Building" in 2004 and as a "Settlement Building Group" in 2011 [^14].

The Rainbow Village represents another model of preservation. Located in Nantun, Taichung, it sits near the Gan Cheng Sixth Village but is not an official Ministry of National Defense village; rather, it consists of houses built by veterans themselves. In 1979, veteran Huang Yong-fu bought one of these houses, and later, the area was slated for demolition due to a rezoning plan. In August 2008, to pass the time and leave a memorial before demolition, he began painting murals on the walls. In 2010, teachers and students from Ling Tung University and Hungkuang University launched the "Rescue Rainbow Village" campaign; that September, the Taichung City Government decided to preserve it as the "Rainbow Arts Park" [^15].

> **💡 Did You Know?**  
> Huang Yong-fu was born in 1924 and was 84 years old when he began painting in 2008. After the village became an internet sensation, the Taichung City Bureau of Cultural Affairs—considering his unfamiliarity with the web—outsourced the creation of an official website and utilized volunteers to help. They also sold cultural creative souvenirs on-site and online to fund the maintenance of the paintings and buildings [^15]. An old man's paintbrush unexpectedly created a new model for village preservation.

**These two cases demonstrate the multiple paths of _juancun_ preservation: academic discourse, community mobilization, internet dissemination, and policy shifts—all are indispensable.**

## Contemporary Transformation of the Village Spirit

### From Space to Memory

While most physical villages have disappeared, the "village spirit" has found new carriers in the digital age:

- **Facebook Groups**: "XX Village Alumni Association" recreates virtual communities.
- **Oral History Projects**: Using video to record and preserve the stories of village elders.
- **3D Digital Reconstruction**: Vanished villages are reborn in virtual spaces.
- **Cultural Creative Industries**: Village elements become popular themes for nostalgic products.

**To some extent, these virtual communities recreate the sense of community found in physical villages.** But whether this "memory of the village" is still a "true village" depends on the individual.

### Taiwan's Model of Diverse Identity

Looking back at over seventy years of history, the greatest inspiration may be this: **Cultural identity does not have to be a zero-sum game.** A person can simultaneously be a child of the _juancun_, a Taiwanese, a Chinese person, and a global citizen; these identities do not exclude one another.

The _juancun_ experience also challenges the notion of a monolithic culture. Cultures from all over China recombined in Taiwan, producing a new culture that is both familiar and strange. Beef noodles, _lu wei_ (braised snacks), and _juancun_ cuisine are not pure representations of any single tradition, but innovative results of "cultural hybridization."

**In the globalized 21st century, the multicultural experiment of the _juancun_ may be a vital experience that Taiwan offers to the world.**

## What the Bamboo Fences Enclosed Was More Than Just Homes

> **✦** A bamboo fence enclosed not just the new homes of nearly 500,000 people, but also one of the most complex and rich strands of DNA in Taiwan's cultural gene pool.

The villages may have vanished, but they left behind more than just nostalgia; they left an ability to face change: rebuilding lives under the harshest conditions, sowing culture on unfamiliar land, and maintaining hope in an uncertain future.

**Hardship and mutual aid, adaptation and persistence, exile and settlement—these seemingly contradictory traits form the core of the _juancun_ spirit.** It reminds us that culture is not a static display in a museum, but a living force present in every stir of a frying pan, every dialect spoken, and every story passed down.

As we move freely through Taiwan's diverse culture today—eating _lei cha_ (pounded tea) in Hakka villages, listening to ancient melodies in Indigenous tribes, or buying pepper buns at a _juancun_ night market—we are actually experiencing an experiment that began over seventy years ago: **how to turn difference into richness, exile into belonging, and a foreign land into a home.**

This is the most precious legacy the villages left to Taiwan: an optimism that believes "culture can start anew," and the wisdom to "remain oneself amidst change."
