# Social Movements and Civic Participation

## 30-Second Overview

Taiwan is a society with vibrant social movements. From the movement to lift martial law in the 1980s, the 1990 Wild Lily student movement, the 2014 Sunflower Movement, to the 2019 legalization of same-sex marriage, social movements have been a key force driving Taiwan's democratization and social progress. Taiwan's social movements are characterized by peaceful rationality, intergenerational transmission, and issue diversity, demonstrating the maturity and vitality of civil society.

These movements span politics, environmental protection, labor, gender, and other domains, appearing in different forms across eras, but all leaving concrete institutional traces in Taiwan's democratic process.

Most of Taiwan's large-scale social movements have been conducted peacefully, but there are exceptions: the 1988 520 Farmers' Movement ended in violent police-civilian clashes with over 130 arrests[^7].

**Key Movements:** Wild Lily student movement (1990), Sunflower Movement (2014), marriage equality movement, environmental movement, labor movement

## Why It Matters

Taiwan's social movement experience demonstrates diverse pathways for citizen political participation in a democratic society. Unlike mature Western democracies, Taiwan's social movements developed gradually during the democratization process—they are both products of democratization and drivers of democratic deepening.

For the international community, Taiwan's social movement experience provides a concrete case of "how to achieve democratic transition in an East Asian context." Particularly, how to advance political reform while maintaining social stability, and how to oversee government through civic participation. There is no textbook answer to this question; Taiwan's exploratory process itself is the case study.

Amid the global wave of disinformation threats and democratic backsliding, the resilience of Taiwan's civil society has also attracted international scholarly attention.

## Historical Context and Developmental Phases

### Opposition Movements During the Authoritarian Era (1949-1987)

During the martial law period, although political space was restricted, sporadic protest activities still occurred:

**Early Struggles:**

- **Zhongli Incident (1977):** Mass protests triggered by election fraud
- **Kaohsiung Incident (1979):** On December 10, 1979, opposition figures held a political rally in Kaohsiung, which was suppressed by military police, leading to the subsequent arrest of numerous democracy activists
- **Campus Democracy Movement:** University students fighting for autonomy and freedom of speech

These movements accumulated organizational experience and social energy for later democratization.

### Explosion of Social Movements Around the Lifting of Martial Law (1986-1990)

Before and after the lifting of martial law, long-suppressed social energy erupted sequentially, with protest movements on various issues emerging in succession:

**Environmental Movements:**

- **Anti-DuPont Movement (1986-1987):** Lukang, Changhua residents opposed a plant by US-based DuPont; DuPont announced cancellation of the plan in March 1987[^4]
- **Anti-Nuclear Movement:** Demanding halt to construction of the Fourth Nuclear Power Plant

**Labor Movements:**

- **Taoyuan Bus Strike (1988):** In February, the Taoyuan Bus Industrial Union struck, triggering a wave of union organizing and strikes by bus workers across Taiwan[^6]
- **Labor Rights:** Fighting for the three labor rights and job security

**Farmers' Movements:**

- **520 Farmers' Movement (1988):** Over 5,000 farmers from across Taiwan marched north to protest; over 130 arrested[^7]
- **Opposition to Expanded US Agricultural Imports:** At the time, the US demanded opening imports of citrus, turkey, and other agricultural products[^7]

## Major Social Movement Case Studies

### Wild Lily Student Movement (March 1990)

**Background:** Large-scale parliamentary reform and political system reform
**Scale:** Approximately 5,000-6,000 university students participated (peak)
**Location:** Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall Plaza (now Liberty Square)

The Wild Lily student movement was the largest student protest action since the government relocated to Taiwan, with significant impact on advancing political democratization.[^1]

**Four Core Demands:**

1. Dissolve the National Assembly
2. Abolish the Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of National Mobilization for Suppression of the Communist Rebellion
3. Convene a National Affairs Conference
4. Timetable for democratic political system reform

**Movement Characteristics:**

- **Peaceful Rationality:** Emphasized nonviolent resistance, maintained on-site order
- **Media Strategy:** Effectively used media to disseminate demands
- **Cross-Campus Alliance:** Nationwide coordinated action by university students
- **Intellectual Participation:** Professors and scholars publicly expressed support

**Historical Significance:**
The Wild Lily student movement directly drove political system reform, accelerating Taiwan's democratization. After the movement ended, the government committed to reforms, the parliament underwent large-scale re-election, and democratic systems such as direct presidential elections were successively established.

### Sunflower Movement (March 2014)

**Background:** Opposition to the black-box handling of the Cross-Strait Service Trade Agreement (CSSTA)
**Scale:** March 30 Ketagalan Boulevard rally; organizers estimated ~500,000, police estimated ~116,000[^2]
**Location:** Legislative Yuan chamber, Executive Yuan, Liberty Square

The Sunflower Movement demonstrated the new generation's concern for democratic quality and Taiwan's subjectivity.

**Core Demands:**

1. Return the Cross-Strait Service Trade Agreement
2. Enact a Cross-Strait Agreement Oversight Law
3. Legislate first, review later
4. Convene a Citizens' Constitutional Conference[^8]

**Movement Characteristics:**

- **Occupation of the Legislative Yuan:** First action to occupy the parliamentary chamber
- **Internet Mobilization:** Extensive use of social media and livestreaming
- **Cross-Generational Participation:** Student-led with cross-generational support
- **International Attention:** Extensive international media coverage

**Social Impact:**

- **Political Impact:** Influenced the 2014 local elections and 2016 presidential election
- **Civic Consciousness:** Elevated political participation among the younger generation
- **Democratic Deepening:** Drove government information transparency and civic participation mechanisms

### Marriage Equality Movement (2013-2019)

**Issue:** Legalization of same-sex marriage
**Key Events:** Constitutional interpretation by Grand Justices, referendums, legislation

Taiwan's marriage equality movement, after years of effort, made Taiwan the first jurisdiction in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage in 2019.

**Movement Trajectory:**

In 2013, legislators proposed marriage equality bills. In December 2016, organizers estimated 200,000-250,000 people gathered on Ketagalan Boulevard in support of same-sex marriage[^9], pushing the issue to the national forefront. In 2017, Grand Justices' Interpretation No. 748 declared existing laws unconstitutional[^5], creating the constitutional basis for legislation.

- **2018:** Pro-marriage equality side lost in referendums
- **2019:** _Act for Implementation of Judicial Yuan Interpretation No. 748_ passed

**Movement Strategies:**

- **Legal Pathways:** Legislative and judicial relief
- **Social Education:** Long-term social communication and education
- **International Linkages:** Collaboration with international human rights organizations
- **Diverse Voices:** Uniting supporters from different backgrounds

**Historical Significance:**
Taiwan became the first jurisdiction in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage, demonstrating Taiwan's progressive values and human rights protections.[^3]

## Development of the Environmental Movement

Taiwan's environmental movement began with anti-pollution struggles in the 1980s and rapidly expanded after the lifting of martial law into a civic force encompassing conservation, anti-nuclear, and climate issues.

### Early Environmental Movement (1980s-1990s)

Starting in the mid-1980s, environmental issues became one of the earliest protest arenas for Taiwan's civil society. In 1986, Lukang, Changhua residents launched the anti-DuPont movement; DuPont announced cancellation of its Lukang plant plan in March 1987, becoming Taiwan's first case where an environmental protest led a foreign investor to terminate an investment plan[^4]. The anti-nuclear movement advanced concurrently. The late-1990s movement to save the Cilan cypress forest saw its first street parade in December 1998, bringing forest conservation to the streets[^10].

### Contemporary Environmental Movement

The focus of contemporary environmental movements has shifted from anti-pollution to climate change, energy transition, and environmental justice. The 2025 renewable energy discussions and electricity policy debates likewise bear traces of civic movement participation.

## Labor Movements and Social Rights

Labor issues were among the earliest mobilization arenas for Taiwan's post-martial law civil society, with demands ranging from basic organizing rights to working-hour protections and minimum wage, covering the needs of different generations of workers.

The substantive implementation of union organizing and strike rights only gained legal support after the protest waves of the 1980s-1990s.

### Labor Rights Movements

Taiwan's labor movement began by fighting for the most basic organizing rights. Energy accumulated in the late 1980s, with the 1988 Taoyuan Bus Strike driving bus workers across Taiwan to organize unions[^6]. Factory closure workers fighting for severance and pensions, the 2012 Hualong Textile Union's 101-day strike over long-unpaid wages[^6], and post-2016 flight attendant strikes in the aviation industry are all important cases where labor movements left concrete impacts. Working-hour reform has been a long-term struggle: the Labor Standards Act was amended in a third reading in December 2016, implementing "one fixed day off, one flexible rest day"; the Act was amended again in January 2018, relaxing the monthly overtime cap and shift intervals[^11].

### Major Labor Movements

- **Hualong Strike (2012):** Textile workers struck for 101 days over unpaid wages
- **Factory Closure Workers' Movement:** Fighting for severance and pensions
- **Flight Attendant Strikes:** Fighting for improved working conditions

## Characteristics and Patterns of Taiwan's Social Movements

### Characteristics of Taiwan's Social Movements

**1. Peaceful Rationality**
Taiwan's social movements predominantly adopt peaceful protest methods, with relatively few violent clashes. Participants value "rational discourse" and "peaceful expression," a culture that helps maintain social stability.

**2. Intergenerational Transmission**
From the Wild Lily generation to the Sunflower generation, Taiwan's social movements display clear intergenerational transmission. The experience and ideals of older activists are passed to younger generations, creating continuity in movement culture.

**3. Issue Diversity**
Taiwan's social movements cover politics, environment, labor, gender, human rights, and other issues, reflecting the diverse needs and concerns of civil society.

**4. Internet-Era Characteristics**
New-generation social movements make full use of the internet and social media for mobilization, communication, and discourse, demonstrating digital-era movement characteristics.

### Organizational Models and Mobilization Strategies

**NGO Role:** Non-governmental organizations support issue energy through professional advocacy and long-term cultivation
**Student Organizations:** University student clubs form the foundation of many social movements
**Cross-Sector Alliances:** Different groups form alliances on specific issues
**Media Strategy:** Skillful use of both traditional and new media to disseminate messages

## Relationship Between Social Movements and Politics

A subtle symbiotic relationship exists between Taiwan's social movements and electoral politics: movements create issue pressure, parties absorb votes, and institutions adjust accordingly.

### Impact on Politics

Social movements' impact on Taiwan politics is not fleeting street noise but repeatedly converts into concrete legislative and electoral outcomes. The 2014 local elections after the Sunflower Movement, and the pro- and anti-mobilization in the marriage equality referendum, clearly show how social movements tug at party strategies and vote structures.

### Institutionalized Participation

Civic participation has gradually become institutionalized. The government holds public hearings before major policy decisions, and the referendum system allows citizens to vote directly on specific issues. Though imperfect, these mechanisms provide formal channels for street movements to enter the system.

## Maturation of Civil Society

### NGO Development

Taiwan has an active NGO network covering human rights, environment, gender, labor, and other issue domains. Organizations such as the Taiwan Association for Human Rights, Taiwan Alliance to Promote Civil Partnership Rights, Green Citizens' Action Alliance, Awakening Foundation, Taiwan Tongzhi Hotline Association, and Taiwan Labor Front have each built long-term advocacy capacity in specific fields, forming the organizational foundation sustaining social movements.

### Civic Participation Culture

Taiwan's civic participation forms have transcended street protests. Volunteer culture, social enterprises, crowdfunding, and online policy discussion platforms (such as vTaiwan) together constitute a digital-era civic participation ecology, enabling more people to find positions for action both within and outside the system.

## Challenges and Reflections

The vitality of Taiwan's civil society is evident, but it also faces several structural dilemmas worthy of serious attention.

### Current Challenges

Proliferation of disinformation and echo-chamber effects polarize issue discussions; different generations have clear divergences on "what constitutes correct participation"; and Blue-Green political barriers force some social movements to take sides, making it difficult to maintain non-partisan stances. How to preserve the independence of civic movements amid partisan politics is a persistent challenge for Taiwan's civil society.

### Future Development

Digital participation platforms, cross-issue strategic alliances, and linkages with international civil society are possible directions for Taiwan's social movements. How to balance institutional innovation with street mobilization remains under exploration.

## International Comparison and Distinctiveness

### East Asian Context

Compared to Japan's inward-looking social movements or South Korea's radicalized protests, Taiwan's social movements display a character of rational moderation with visible effectiveness.

### Democratization Experience

Taiwan's social movement experience provides an important case of "gradual democratic transition," demonstrating how to achieve political reform while maintaining social stability.

### Influence on Hong Kong

Taiwan's social movement experience significantly influenced Hong Kong's Occupy Central and Umbrella Movement, demonstrating the possibility of Chinese societies practicing democracy.

## Cultural Significance of Social Movements

### Cultivation of Civic Consciousness

Social movements are an important channel for civic education; through participation, citizens learn democratic values, public participation, and social responsibility.

### Debate on Social Values

Social movements promote social discussion and reflection on different values, driving social value renewal and progress.

### Deepening of Democratic Culture

Through social movements, Taiwan society has shaped a democratic culture of "pluralistic respect," "rational debate," and "peaceful expression."

## Lessons for Foreign Observers

### Diversity of Democratic Practice

Taiwan's experience shows that democratic practice can take different forms and paths, without needing to fully imitate Western models.

### Positive Function of Social Movements

In mature democratic societies, social movements are healthy forms of political participation, allowing democratic systems to self-correct and deepen.

### Importance of Civil Society

Strong civil society is a crucial foundation for democratic consolidation, requiring long-term cultural cultivation and institutional support.

## Further Reflections

Taiwan's social movement experience demonstrates how a society can achieve self-reform and progress through civic participation. This experience holds profound reference value for understanding the operating mechanisms of modern democratic societies and how to seek consensus in pluralistic societies.

Going forward, Taiwan's social movements face new challenges of the digital era, globalization, and political polarization. How to maintain movements' persuasiveness and legitimacy in a changing environment warrants long-term observation.
