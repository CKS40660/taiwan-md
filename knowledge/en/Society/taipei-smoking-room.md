_Ximen MRT Exit 6, Taiwan’s first outdoor negative‑pressure smoking room: a black‑framed transparent glass box standing on the sidewalk. Photo: Hua Hua Daily / Reporter Chang Hsiang‑ying (fair use editorial commentary)._

> **30‑second overview:** Taipei has written “principle‑prohibited, exception‑allowed” into the streets: outdoor smoking is banned throughout Ximending and the Zhongshan area, while the city has erected Taiwan’s first batch of outdoor negative‑pressure smoking booths, using exhaust fans to create an indoor‑outdoor pressure differential so smoke only flows in, not out. Supporters say people can finally breathe easily; even anti‑tobacco groups oppose it, arguing that the more comfortable the booth, the further it strays from the original goal of getting people to quit. A visible glass box holds a fight that has lasted forty years and has now moved outdoors.

## A Transparent Box Parked by the Arcade

On May 7 2026, between the Chengdu Yang‑fang ice stand and the original flagship store of Taiwan Salt‑Fried Chicken, a transparent box appeared on the sidewalk outside Exit 6 of Ximen MRT. About 16 square metres—someone on Dcard measured it and said it is “almost the size of a parking space.” Inside are four filter layers (oil filter, primary air filter, activated carbon, PM2.5); smoke enters, gets washed layer by layer, then exits through an angled vent. Opening hours run from 8 a.m. to midnight, with a sign at the door: “No entry for those under 20 or pregnant women.” This is Taiwan’s first outdoor negative‑pressure smoking room[^1].

The term “negative pressure” sounds like lab jargon, but it is actually a counter‑intuitive physical phenomenon. As early as 2008, when the Sheraton Hotel installed a qualified indoor smoking room, its PR manager Bai Jing‑hui described it to TV reporters this way: “The feeling of negative pressure is that when you open the door, the air inside does not rush out; instead, outside air rushes in.”[^2] The exhaust fan lowers the air pressure inside the box relative to the outside, so when the door opens, outside air flows in and the smoke is trapped inside. This principle is the same as that used in negative‑pressure isolation wards for infectious diseases in hospitals.

A city aiming to become “smoke‑free” first builds a glass box for smoking on its busiest street corner—seems contradictory, doesn’t it? That breathing‑transparent box is actually Taipei’s most concrete embodiment so far of re‑inserting a legal but harmful cigarette into the public‑space contract, as the city negotiates the terms of that agreement. Following the smoke from office ashtrays to this glass box reveals a city that has spent forty years on the issue and still hasn’t finished the discussion.

Yet this box has been misbehaving from the start. After just three days of use, one of its manual doors wouldn’t seal properly and kept popping open; the Public Works Bureau said it had already adjusted the door track[^3]. A booth that claims to maintain negative pressure through sealing can’t even shut its door. This tiny gap perfectly foreshadows the later debate: the claimed seal versus the real leak—an endless tug‑of‑war.

## Air Used to Be Free

To understand why a street‑side smoking booth became a big deal, we must go back to the era when smoke was just part of the air. Before 2009, offices, trains, KTVs, and restaurants in Taiwan were filled with cigarette smoke. The Tobacco and Wine Monopoly’s “Changshou” cigarette, launched in 1958, remained the market leader for years and still accounted for 76 % of Taiwan’s cigarette sales in 1987—that is, three out of every four smokers smoked Changshou[^4]. The Songshan Tobacco Factory, begun in 1937 by the Taiwan Governor‑General Monopoly Bureau, at its peak employed two thousand workers and produced over NT$21 billion annually, operating until 1998[^5]. Tobacco was once a state‑run business, and smoking was the background hum of daily life on this island.

![Songshan Tobacco Factory cigarette‑production plant north side, modern cigarette‑rolling factory built in 1937 by the Taiwan Governor‑General Monopoly Bureau, later became the production base of the Tobacco and Wine Monopoly](/article-images/society/songshan-tobacco-factory-north.webp)

_Songshan Tobacco Factory cigarette‑production plant: built in 1937 by the Taiwan Governor‑General Monopoly Bureau, taken over after the war by the Tobacco and Wine Monopoly, operated until 1998 and now part of the Songshan Cultural Creative Park. Tobacco was once the state’s business. Photo: Shr‑person Meng‑zi / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY‑SA 4.0._

This background hum began to shift when one person had a lung lobe removed. Yan Dao started smoking at age 12; at 52 he had his right upper lobe excised due to tobacco‑related disease, and after leaving the hospital he secretly vowed to work on tobacco‑harm prevention[^6]. On May 19 1984, his friend Dong Zhiying, to thank him for resolving a legal dispute, offered US 2.5 million (about NT$100 million at the era’s exchange rate); Yan Dao instead suggested using the money to found a foundation dedicated to tobacco‑harm prevention[^7]. Thus the Dong Foundation was born.

That same April, actor Sun Yueh was filming _Old Mo’s Second Spring_ in southern Taiwan. After a take, he habitually reached for a cigarette to light, saw two uniformed students walking toward him, and thought: “Sun Yueh! Your smoking harms others—do you still want to smoke?” He quit his 38‑year smoking habit, volunteered at the Dong Foundation, and promoted a slogan many still remember: “Everyone has the right to refuse secondhand smoke.”[^8] That sentence shifted the question from “Should you quit smoking for your own health?” to “Does your smoke harm the people around you?” This reframing is the starting point of all outdoor‑smoking bans forty years later.

From that slogan to law took thirteen years. The Tobacco Harm Prevention Act was promulgated in March 1997 and took effect in September 1997; it was amended in 2007, and from January 11 2009 indoor public and workplaces with three or more people went completely smoke‑free; on March 22 2023 it was expanded again to include kindergartens, colleges, bars and nightclubs, completely banned e‑cigarettes, and raised the legal smoking age to 20[^9]. Each expansion shrank the permissible smoking area by one notch.

```tw-timeline
Forty years of tobacco harm prevention: the permissible smoking area shrinks ring by ring
1984 | Dong Foundation founded | Yan Dao established it after lung loss; Sun Yueh promoted “Everyone has the right to refuse secondhand smoke”
1997 | Tobacco Harm Prevention Act takes effect | Promulgated March 19, effective September 19, first regulation of public‑place smoking
2009 | Indoor smoking fully banned | Effective January 11, indoor workplaces with three or more people totally smoke‑free
2023 | Expanded smoking ban, total e‑cigarette ban | Effective March 22, smoking age raised to 20
2026 | Smoke‑free city, outdoor negative‑pressure smoking rooms | First outdoor negative‑pressure smoking room launched May 7
Source: National Laws Database, Ministry of Health and Welfare, Central News Agency
```

The tobacco business also withdrew step by step. Taiwan joined the WTO in 2002, the Tobacco and Wine Monopoly was reorganized into Taiwan Tobacco and Liquor Corporation, ending forty years of monopoly[^10]; in 2016 tobacco farmers and the corporation reached an agreement, the last batch of tobacco leaves was cured in spring 2017, and tobacco‑leaf farming in Taiwan entered history[^11]. Something that once dominated the air and filled the treasury has been gradually pushed out of public space.

![Original Taiwan Provincial Tobacco and Wine Monopoly Bureau building on Nanchang Road, Taipei, red‑brick Baroque structure, now the Taiwan Tobacco and Liquor Corporation](/article-images/society/taiwan-tobacco-monopoly-bureau-building.webp)

## Indoors Won, Outdoors Still Unsettled

By the 2020s this campaign looks like a clear victory on paper. The smoking rate among adults aged 18 and over fell from 21.9 % in 2008 to 12.8 % in 2024—a drop of over forty percent, the lowest since 1990[^12]. Secondhand‑smoke exposure in indoor workplaces also dropped from 32.1 % in 2008 to 22.1 % in 2020[^13]. Hundreds of thousands fewer smokers mean ashtrays have practically vanished from offices.

But split the secondhand‑smoke numbers and you see two opposite trends. Indoor public‑place secondhand‑smoke exposure plunged from 27.8 % in 2008 to 3.2 % in 2024—indoor battle essentially won. Yet outdoor locations without a smoking ban rose from 36.2 % in 2008 to a peak of 58.5 % in 2013, then fell to 48.9 % in 2024, still leaving nearly half exposed[^12]. Smoke hasn’t disappeared; it’s just been pushed outside.

```tw-line
Two trends of secondhand smoke: indoor almost cleared, outdoor still near half (exposure rate %)
Year | Indoor public places | Outdoor non‑ban locations
2008 | 27.8 | 36.2
2024 | 3.2  | 48.9
Source: Health Promotion Administration Adult Smoking Behavior Survey
```

Ask where people most often encounter this smoke, and the answer is specific. In the 2024 survey, the most common places to encounter secondhand smoke were “on the road, on the street, in arcades and other outdoor passageways,” accounting for 27.9 %, far ahead of second place—parks (7.7 %) and convenience‑store arcades (7.7 %)[^14]. Put another way, indoor smoking bans pushed smokers out the door, and the arcade became the new smoke corridor. Walking through Ximending, you are forced to become a “breath‑holding expert”—the data show no exaggeration.

> 📝 **Curator’s note:** Worth pausing to examine the underlying mechanics of this policy. Previously outdoor policy meant “draw a no‑smoking zone”: assume everywhere is smokable, then carve out a few no‑smoke spots. The current administration’s smoke‑free city flips that presumption: outdoors, smoking is prohibited by default, and only “listed” designated spots are allowed. On the same sidewalk, the default shifts from “smokable” to “non‑smokable.” Adding more signs is merely cosmetic; what’s truly rewritten is who owns the city’s air.

## Principle‑Prohibited, Exception‑Allowed

After the flip, the remaining question is where the few remaining smokers should go. The city’s answer is “distribution management”: ban outdoor smoking outright, but guide smokers to designated smoking areas or rooms[^15]. Starting June 1, Ximending and the Zhongshan district went completely smoke‑free outdoors, with violations punishable by up to NT$10,000; simultaneously the city’s designated smoking areas grew to 183 spots, then to 201 by the end of May, among which this batch of Taiwan’s first outdoor negative‑pressure smoking rooms is included[^16].

The negative‑pressure principle was explained clearly by Lin Hui‑zhong, chief secretary of the Public Works Bureau’s New Engineering Division, on the launch day: “The facility adopts ‘negative‑pressure airflow control technology,’ using exhaust fans and pressure adjustment to make indoor pressure lower than outdoor atmospheric pressure, creating a physical barrier where air can only flow inward and cannot escape outward, effectively reducing smoke leakage.”[^15] These boxes vary in size; the largest, on Zhonghua Road, holds 16 people[^1]; a smaller one, such as the one at Exit 4 of Ximen Station, is 2 × 8 metres, and the Research, Development and Evaluation Commission chair Yin Wei says “more than ten people can stand inside at once”[^17].

Why does this design become “negative pressure” rather than Osaka’s fully enclosed smoking booth? Behind it lies a technical detour. Taipei originally wanted to copy Osaka’s sealed model, but the central government blocked it with the reasoning: “Four sealed walls count as indoor, and the Tobacco Harm Prevention Act forbids smoking indoors.”[^18] Hence the airflow‑control, non‑fully‑sealed “negative‑pressure” design emerged as a workaround to avoid being classified as indoor. The technology itself isn’t new. Japan’s 2020 Health Promotion Act already set a legal baseline for enclosed smoking rooms: entrance‑exit wind speed must be at least 0.2 m/s; Tokyo even added stricter supplementary rules[^19]. Taipei’s true innovation isn’t the negative‑pressure filtration itself, but turning it into a free piece of street furniture that stands in the city.

As for cost, the city has not released a itemized budget. In legislative questioning it cited a planning estimate of roughly NT$1.5 million per unit, projecting total expenses of about NT$100 million by end‑June[^20]. On the other side, the National Congress of Parent‑Teacher Associations chairman Tao Lian‑sheng questioned the cost‑effectiveness from a cost‑benefit angle: outdoor smoking rooms require independent compartments, independent air‑conditioning, negative‑pressure design, and automatic doors; “if the ‘cannot smell smoke’ effect isn’t achieved, yet tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of NT dollars are still spent, the cost‑benefit is clearly poor.”[^21] One set is the city’s planning baseline; the other is a parent‑group’s critical estimate. Though the numbers differ in nature, both point to the same question: Is this box worth it?

## Even the Anti‑Smoking Camp Is Fighting

If the only opposition came from parents complaining about the cost, the picture would be simple. What makes this complicated is that the anti‑smoking camp itself has broken into open argument — not over _whether_ to fight tobacco, but _how_.

The Taiwan Anti‑Smoking Alliance and the Dong Foundation hold a clear line: they oppose building enclosed, negative‑pressure smoking rooms outdoors. Lin Ching‑li, director of the Dong Foundation’s Tobacco Control Center, argues for an “inside‑out” approach: “First achieve one‑hundred‑percent indoor smoke‑free, then designate outdoor smoking points.” She states bluntly: “A smoke‑free city should make things inconvenient for smokers, not blanket the city with smoking kiosks.”[^22] In their logic, the more comfortable the smoking room, the more it paves the way for smoking — drifting further from the original goal of helping people quit. This isn’t a new position; as early as 2011 the Dong Foundation criticized Taoyuan Airport’s plan to bring back indoor smoking rooms.

```tw-versus
Harm reduction: corral the smoke into a box | Reduction: make smoking harder and harder
Acknowledge some people still smoke, give them a corner that doesn’t bother others | Goal: keep driving the smoking rate down
Centralized management of smell and butts, cleaner streetscape | The less convenient, the more likely people smoke less or quit outright
Current city government’s diversion logic | Anti‑smoking groups’ and part of the medical community’s stance
Source: Taipei City Council interpellation and parent‑group cost‑benefit estimates (CNA etc.) + Taiwan Anti‑Smoking Alliance and Dong Foundation positions[^20][^21][^22]
```

NTU Hospital Family Medicine attending physician and Taiwan Medical Alliance for Tobacco Control Secretary‑General Kuo Fei‑ran goes a layer deeper. In a contributed article he cites the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, noting that no ventilation, air‑filtration equipment, or designated smoking room can prevent secondhand‑smoke exposure: “There is no safe level of exposure to secondhand smoke.”[^23] His sharpest line targets the side‑effect of the box’s very existence.

```tw-quote
Strongly support a smoke‑free city, but don’t set up outdoor enclosed smoking rooms; don’t claim that because a smoking room has negative pressure and filtration there’s no secondhand smoke; don’t promote a wrong concept for a good‑hearted purpose.
Kuo Fei‑ran | NTU Hospital Family Medicine physician, Secretary‑General of Taiwan Medical Alliance for Tobacco Control, 2026
```

> 📝 **Curator’s Note**: Kuo’s remark hides a mechanism that is easy to miss. A futuristic‑looking glass box touting “four‑stage filtration” implies “equipment equals safety”: when you see the smoke sucked into a high‑tech box, you instinctively feel the secondhand‑smoke problem has been handled. Scientifically, that reassurance doesn’t hold. The 2006 U.S. Surgeon General’s Report concluded that even separately enclosed, separately exhausted negative‑pressure smoking rooms cannot stop secondhand smoke from seeping into adjacent areas.[^24] The WHO and ASHRAE take the same position. The glass house is both a considerate compromise and a potential guardrail that lets people lower their guard — both things can be true at the same time.

## The Kyoto Study That Was Actually Measuring Something Else

The debate has reached the point where both sides bring out evidence, and the study most often cited happens to be a perfect example of “the same data being described as two different things.”

The Anti‑Smoking Alliance quoted at a press conference a 2023 study from Kyoto Women’s University finding that “high‑spec‑equipment enclosed smoking rooms” had PM2.5 concentrations exceeding air‑quality warning levels, with readings even 5 metres outside the entrance exceeding standards[^25]. That sounds like a direct hammer blow to negative‑pressure smoking rooms. But tracking down the original paper reveals its title is actually “Passive Smoking Conditions Around the Outdoor Smoking Area of Kyoto Station,” and the subject was an open‑air smoking corner at Kyoto Station that has partial dividers but no doors or ceiling; the study measured PM2.5 at distances of 3 metres and 10 metres to the east[^26]. It did **not** measure an enclosed room; it measured an open‑air spot with a roof overhang and four‑sided ventilation.

That gap is significant. A sealed negative‑pressure room and an open‑air smoking shelter are two different physical structures. Professor Yamada Hiroshi of the Industrial Medical University, in an interview, directly called that Kyoto Station spot an “open‑air smoking corner” and criticized installing such a corner under a large roof as unreasonable, saying it should be at least 25 metres from non‑smokers’ flow[^27]. Put differently, the anti‑tobacco side wanted to use this study to prove “even enclosed rooms can’t block smoke,” but the study itself shows “even a sheltered open‑air corner lets smoke escape.”

Both sides cite the same research, yet they are measuring different things. Interestingly, this information gap itself loops back to the same honest reminder: the problem isn’t only whether there is enclosure, but whether the airflow design is adequate—whether sealed or open, smoke will linger if the airflow is off. To truly validate a negative‑pressure sealed room, one must return to the consistent conclusion of the CDC, WHO, and engineering societies, not rely on a secondhand‑described outdoor study that’s gone astray. The anti‑tobacco position is correct, but the evidence they picked is wrong—a common occurrence in public debate.

## Whose Arcade?

Turning the lens from data and papers back to the street reveals a more complicated crowd. Those invited into the glass box, or those expelled from the arcade, are not a monolith.

![Ximen pedestrian zone street corner, Taiwan’s first outdoor negative‑pressure smoking room sits on this densely crowded sidewalk](/article-images/society/ximending-street-crossing.webp)

_Ximen pedestrian zone: the first batch of Taiwan’s outdoor negative‑pressure smoking rooms is located in this densest‑traffic area. Who has the right to light a cigarette here is the core of the debate. Photo: Bigmorr / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY‑SA 4.0._

Some welcome it. In a street interview by the China Times in Ximending, a smoker with experience said “It’s good for non‑smokers—it won’t affect them inhaling secondhand smoke”; a female smoker said she used to encounter kids or parents pushing strollers when smoking on the roadside, but now having a fixed smoking area reduces mutual friction[^28]. Others feel pushed too far. A smoker complained on Dcard that the smoking room is even smaller than imagined—almost just a parking‑space size—wondering if they have to queue for a smoke and still be bound by opening hours[^29]. The city replied that Japanese negative‑pressure smoking rooms also close at 10 or 11 p.m., and that all smoking rooms are within a three‑to‑five‑minute walk of each other[^30].

Interestingly, a reply to that “like a parking‑space” complaint said: “Do you know how non‑smokers feel normally?”[^29] One sentence puts both grievances side‑by‑side: smokers feel they’re being shoved into a tiny, distant box, while non‑smokers feel they’ve been squeezed by smoke for decades and never had much room to stand. The same glass box shows one side cramped space, the other side belated fairness.

Stepping back further reveals that smoking itself follows a class line. Among adult males, those with junior‑high‑school education or lower have a smoking rate of 35.8 %, three times the 11.9 % rate for those with college or higher[^31]. In the 30‑to‑49 age bracket, the rate for junior‑high‑or‑lower climbs to 50 %, nearly double the 27 % rate for college‑or‑higher.

```tw-bars
Lower education, higher smoking: junior‑high‑or‑below males smoke three times as much as college‑or‑above (%)
*Junior‑high‑or‑below | 35.8 | 3 × college‑or‑above
College‑or‑above | 11.9
Source: Ministry of Health and Welfare (adult male smoking rate, six‑year anniversary of Tobacco Harm Prevention Act)
```

> 📝 **Curator’s note:** This three‑percent‑gap line conceals an unpleasant cycle. A large share of the tobacco‑health surcharge—levied per thousand cigarettes—flows back to subsidize the disadvantaged: tobacco taxes pay health‑insurance premiums for 446,000 economically struggling people and help keep the nation’s 13 public relief institutions running[^32]. Put another way, the lowest‑SES group smokes the most, and the extra tax they pay per pack circles back to subsidize the medical safety net that includes themselves. This isn’t a simple right‑or‑wrong formula; readers should chew on it: when we usher smokers into a glass box, those who end up inside are often the people who already occupy the city’s margins.

Local shopkeepers and village heads worry about more practical management issues. The Ximen Pedestrian Zone Street‑Area Development Promotion Association chair Liu Jia‑xin responded to the smoke‑free commercial zone call, but also said “Daytime probably won’t bring management problems; I’m more worried about nighttime”; Ximen Village head Ye Min‑hui pointed out the many homeless people in Ximending, fearing “future management will indeed be the biggest challenge”[^33]. These are anticipatory remarks heard in interviews, not yet‑occurred facts, yet they truthfully reflect the everyday issues a policy that concentrates people in a fixed space will face.

## Tokyo Built It Too, Then What?

Taipei isn’t the only city wrestling with this. Diverting smokers into special spaces has long been a shared language among many Asian cities—just with different local flavors.

Japan moved earliest and most fragmented. Tokyo’s Chiyoda Ward enacted an ordinance in October 2002 to fine street smoking ¥2,000, one of Japan’s earliest street‑smoking penalties; Shibuya Ward followed in 2019, banning smoking in all public places[^34]. The fragmentation lies in the fact that the Tokyo Metropolis has no citywide rule; its 23 wards each set their own, so the same cigarette may be legal on one street and become a ¥2,000 violation upon crossing into the next ward. By contrast, Taipei currently applies a single standard city‑wide, avoiding that cross‑ward confusion.

![Outdoor designated smoking area in Ohori Park, Fukuoka, semi‑open wooden‑slatted wooden‑frame design with a SMOKING AREA sign](/article-images/society/ohori-park-smoking-area-fukuoka.webp)

_Fukuoka Ohori Park’s outdoor smoking area uses a wooden lattice to create a semi‑open corner, quite unlike Taipei’s fully glass‑walled, negative‑pressure‑reliant box. Photo: Hirho / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0._

Other cities have their own formulas, but differing currencies and mechanisms make direct size comparisons inadvisable.

```tw-stat
NT$2,000–10,000 | Taipei Ximending/Zhongshan district outdoor smoking violation | Effective June 1 2026
¥2,000 | Tokyo Chiyoda & Shibuya wards street smoking | Fine, paid on the spot
HK$3,000 | Hong Kong statutory no‑smoking area | Effective Jan 1 2026, raised from HK$1,500
€135 | France outdoor areas frequented by children | Effective July 1 2025
Source: Various municipal / health‑authority announcements
```

Singapore’s approach paints “yellow boxes” on the ground of hawker centres and coffee‑shop exteriors; smokers must stand entirely inside the box. The bustling Orchard Road corridor has been a no‑smoking zone since 1 Jan 2019, with only a handful of designated smoking spots permitted[^35]. Hong Kong raised its statutory no‑smoking‑area fixed fine from HK$1,500 to HK$3,000 on 1 Jan 2026 and added a “no smoking while queuing” rule[^36]. France went further, banning smoking from July 1 2025 in parks, beaches, school gates, and other child‑frequented outdoor spaces[^37]. The smoking‑free frontier is creeping into ever‑more granular everyday scenes worldwide.

Taipei’s own wording is “nationwide first,” meaning the first outdoor negative‑pressure smoking room in Taiwan; English‑ and Chinese‑language media have not called it a “world first”[^38]. This modest claim holds up: as of the current verification scope, no other government has installed this kind of mechanical negative‑pressure booth on a street. As for the oft‑cited warning “Tokyo 80 % violation,” tracing the source leads to a Kobe University team’s article in _BMC Public Health_ measuring the non‑compliance rate of indoor restaurants and bars in Tokyo, Osaka, and Aomori prefecture under the 2020 smoking ban (78.4 %), not the violation rate of outdoor smoking spots[^39]. The two aren’t directly comparable, but both point to the same reminder: no matter how finely the rules are written, the real test always comes at enforcement.

## Corpse‑Vape Pods, and the Unfinished Ledger

The battlefield of tobacco is still shifting. While Taipei debates the glass box for paper cigarettes, the next showdown has already changed opponents.

As paper cigarettes fade, e‑cigarettes have quietly grown among teenagers. Junior‑high e‑cigarette use rose from 1.9 % in 2018 to 3.9 % in 2021; senior‑vocational‑high use rose from 3.4 % to 8.8 % over the same period, while paper‑cigarette use among that cohort declined[^40]. Taiwan banned e‑cigarettes outright in 2023; heated‑tobacco products take a different route, passing health‑risk evaluation and becoming legal for sale in convenience stores starting October 2025[^41]. The latest 2026 move, spurred by the so‑called “corpse‑vape pods” containing etizolab, saw the Executive Yuan pass a draft amendment to the Tobacco Harm Prevention Act on June 25 that would raise penalties for possession or use of e‑cigarettes from NT$2,000–10,000 to NT$30,000–100,000, make manufacturing and trafficking a criminal offense punishable by up to seven years imprisonment, though the bill is still under legislative review and has not yet passed third reading[^42].

![Modern disposable e‑cigarette (pod style); Taiwan has banned manufacture, import, sale, and use of e‑cigarettes since 2023](/article-images/society/electronic-cigarette-pod.webp)

_E‑cigarettes are the new front in the tobacco‑harm war: Taiwan banned them in 2023, yet youth use keeps climbing, and now the “corpse‑vape pod” toxin has prompted another round of legal amendments. The cigarette changed its shell, but the fight isn’t over. Photo: Jacek Halicki / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY‑SA 4.0._

Civil society keeps pushing forward. In September 2025 a member of the public submitted a proposal on the Public Policy Online Participation Platform to “ban pedestrian smoking,” arguing that even walking while smoking should be prohibited; it quickly gathered enough signatures[^43]. The places where tobacco can exist have shrunk from indoors to the arcade, and now into that glass box; today even lighting up while walking draws calls for a ban. The available smoking space continues to contract.

Return to the transparent box at Ximen MRT Exit 6. The person inside complains it’s too cramped and has to stub out when the time limit hits; the person outside finally can walk the arcade without holding their breath. The city calls this a thoughtful act of distribution; anti‑tobacco advocates call it a compromising betrayal; family‑medicine doctors warn the biggest danger is that it leads people to think “having filtration means no secondhand smoke.” Four groups look at the same box and see four truths—and perhaps that’s why it’s made of glass: so everyone can see who is watching whom, and who is being watched, all laid bare.

Smoke has never truly vanished. It drifted from office ashtrays to the arcade, then into this breathing glass box; now it’s wearing an e‑cigarette shell and drifting toward the next unfinished fight. Forty years ago, when Yan Dao lost a lung lobe, Taiwan’s air was still free; forty years later, a legal but harmful cigarette has been placed back into the city’s still‑unsettled public‑space contract. That transparent box on Ximen Street isn’t a period—it’s merely the most visible shape of the negotiation so far.
