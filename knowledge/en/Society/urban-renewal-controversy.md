# Urban Renewal Controversy: How Taiwan Handles "Dissenting Owners" After Wenlin Yuan

> **30-second overview:** On March 28, 2012, Taipei City Government demolished two houses of the Wang family at Wenlin Yuan in Shilin under the Urban Renewal Ordinance, becoming the largest controversy in Taiwan's urban renewal history. The Wang family filed eleven appeals and lawsuits, losing ten of them; courts ruled their land was a dead-end lot (袋地) not meeting building line requirements, making it impossible to rebuild if excluded from the renewal zone. In 2013, the Constitutional Court issued Interpretation No. 709, declaring the ordinance's summary review and hearing procedures unconstitutional; the Legislative Yuan subsequently amended the law. In 2014, the Wang family and the developer settled; the building completed in 2016. The case ended, but the systemic problems remain.

---

## One Building and Two Dead-End Lots

The Wenlin Yuan case took place in the Wenlin Road area of Shilin District, Taipei. On October 28, 2002, Taipei City Government designated this block as an urban renewal zone under the Urban Renewal Ordinance, citing reasons including "buildings showing signs of deterioration or decay due to age, poor building arrangement or narrow, winding roads that may impede public transportation or public safety."[^1]

In 2006, local residents surnamed Lai initiated urban renewal under Article 10 of the ordinance. On December 15, 2006, a project outline public hearing was held, excluding buildings that could be legally excluded, such as the Kuo Yuan Yi building facing the street and a Yingyingong temple. An application was submitted in March 2007 and approved by the city government on May 1.[^1] Residents subsequently appointed Leyang Construction as the implementer. On January 4, 2008, a public hearing on the project plan and rights-exchange plan was held, obtaining consent from 73.68% of private landowners and 77.83% of the land area within the renewal unit. After meeting the threshold, approval was requested and granted on June 16, 2009.[^1]

The Wang family was one of two households in the unit that did not sign a consent agreement. Their land consisted of two small parcels—Yangming Section 801 and 803—zoned as a first-class commercial district with an average depth of less than 15 meters and a frontage width of less than 3.5 meters.[^1] According to Taipei City's Urban Renewal Department, these two parcels did not meet the legal average depth requirement for a first-class commercial district and did not comply with "Taipei City's principles for applying for building permits on existing alleys," making it impossible to apply for a building permit independently.[^1]

In other words, this was not a case of "my house faces the main road, so whether to participate in urban renewal is my choice." Under building law, the Wang family's land could only be developed by combining it with neighboring land; part of it also fell within a six-meter no-build zone around the MRT elevated line.[^1] This technical premise is the starting point for understanding the entire controversy: excluding the Wang family from the renewal zone meant these two parcels could never be rebuilt.

## What Happened That Year

The Wang family stated publicly that when the developer first visited in 2006, they declined to participate because their house had just been renovated. The developer proposed an alternative plan to rebuild the two Wang family houses as a semi-detached townhouse, but the two sides reached no consensus, and this record of intent was not registered by the implementer. The Wang family said they did not learn they had been included in the renewal zone until receiving the rights-exchange notice.[^1]

The city government and developer offered a different account. The city government pointed out that from the 2007 application for the project outline through the 2009 approval of the project plan, the dissenting owner never submitted any opinion. The developer presented a certified mail receipt that the Wang family did not deny receiving at a coordination meeting, arguing that the Wang family had attended an information session. The Wang family countered that the receipt showed address errors and signs of alteration, and stated in their appeal that they did not understand the law and did not know they should send a letter to the government to formally decline participation.[^1]

On January 25, 2010, the implementer applied to the city government for forced demolition under Article 36 of the ordinance. Over the following two-plus years, the city government held five officially organized coordination meetings—exceeding the legal requirement. After coordination failed, approval was granted on March 1, 2012, and demolition was executed on March 28.[^1] On demolition day, the Urban Renewal Victims Alliance and other groups mobilized over four hundred supporters to the site. The construction fence was damaged and spray-painted with protest slogans; subsequently, with the Wang family's consent, supporters erected prefabricated houses at the original site to block construction.[^1]

## How the Courts Viewed It

From 2009 to 2013, the Wang family filed eleven appeals and lawsuits against Taipei City Government, losing ten of them.[^1] The key issue in Taipei High Administrative Court Case No. 98-Su-2467 was whether the public hearing notice had been properly served.

The court found that before the January 4, 2008 public hearing, Wang Guoxiong had received the notice through Wang Jiajun, who lived at the same address; Wang Guangshu, Wang Qingquan, and Wang Zhiping's notices were returned unclaimed after the deadline, constituting circumstances attributable to their own fault. Multiple certified mail receipts showed the same address as the one on the Wang family's complaint, leading the court to find that the Wang family's claim of "complete ignorance" violated ordinary experience and common sense.[^1]

On the scope of the renewal zone, the court agreed with the city government's position: the Wang family's land did not meet the building line requirement and was smaller than the minimum buildable area, making it a dead-end lot and irregular parcel. Excluding it from the renewal unit would actually prevent the Wang family from rebuilding, violating current regulations. The Kuo Yuan Yi building and four other households, because they faced the building line, could rebuild independently; after the owners voluntarily withdrew and negotiations were held, the exclusion was procedurally lawful.[^1]

This reasoning contained an easily overlooked implication: the legal issue in this case was never "can the government demolish private homes," but rather "did the Wang family express their non-participation at the correct time and in the correct manner." The Wang family left no formal record of opposition during either the project outline or project plan stages, only raising objections at the rights-exchange stage—procedurally too late.[^1]

## Interpretation No. 709: Procedural Legitimacy

Wenlin Yuan forced the constitutionality of the Urban Renewal Ordinance under scrutiny. On April 22, 2012, the Legislative Yuan completed a joint petition for constitutional interpretation; the Constitutional Court rejected it on June 22, reasoning that legislators should first propose amendments, and only seek interpretation if amendment failed.[^1] The Wang family and other dissenting owners from urban renewal communities filed their own petition. On April 26, 2013, the Constitutional Court issued Interpretation No. 709.

The core of the interpretation concerned procedure: Article 10, Paragraph 1 of the Urban Renewal Ordinance, enacted in 1998, regarding the procedure by which the competent authority approves an urban renewal project outline, failed to establish an appropriate organization for review and did not ensure that interested parties were informed and had timely opportunity to state their views, violating the constitutional requirement for due administrative process. Article 10, Paragraph 2 and Article 19, Paragraph 3 were also declared unconstitutional, with a one-year deadline for correction.[^2][^3]

Legislative Yuan records on this matter were straightforward: "Due to Constitutional Court Interpretation No. 709 issued on April 26, 2013, Articles 10, Paragraphs 1 and 2, and Article 19, Paragraph 3 of this ordinance were declared unconstitutional."[^3] Amendment directions included raising the consent threshold for project outlines and project plans, and transferring the controversial issue of "forced demolition" to court adjudication.[^1] The Executive Yuan submitted an amendment draft on November 7, 2012, though actual amendment was completed by subsequent sessions of the Legislative Yuan.

## Settlement and a Completed Building

On March 14, 2014, Wang Yao-de, son of Wang Guangshu, voluntarily demolished the prefabricated houses. His statement clearly laid out the financial pressure of the later stages of the protest: the court's first-instance ruling found the prefabricated houses constituted unlawful occupation; after losing the appeal, he might face a developer's claim for damages. The counter-guarantee loan that support groups had raised for him might take twenty years to repay.[^1]

On May 28, after losing ten lawsuits and facing a NT$53 million damages claim from the developer, Wang Guangshu signed a settlement agreement with Leyang Construction, which withdrew all lawsuits. The Wenlin Yuan building completed and was handed over in September 2016.[^1]

It is worth noting that the "disaster victims" in this case were not only the Wang family. Consenting owners could not return home during the protest; reports indicated that seven elderly consenting owners passed away before the building's completion.[^1] This was the greatest difference between Wenlin Yuan and typical demolition protests: it was not a two-sided confrontation between government and residents, but a community split in half, with both sides paying a price.

## What the System Left Behind

The Urban Renewal Ordinance was promulgated on November 11, 1998, and took effect on November 13, serving as Taiwan's special law for urban renewal.[^4] According to statistics from the Urban Renewal Research and Development Foundation, from 2000 through the end of 2020, a cumulative 751 reconstruction project cases were approved nationwide under the ordinance, covering a total area of 242.70 hectares, with 150 renovation and maintenance project cases approved.[^4] As of November 13, 2024, the ordinance has undergone twelve amendments.[^4]

After amendment, handling of dissenting owners added a procedural step: the Urban Renewal Review Committee must confirm the reasonableness of the renewal scope, and a hearing must be held in case of dispute. The issue of forced demolition also shifted from "the city government decides" to court adjudication.[^1] These designs responded to the core concerns of Interpretation No. 709, but did not answer a more fundamental question: when the property interests of the majority conflict with the residential wishes of the minority, which side should the law take.

The answer in the Wenlin Yuan case was "majority decision plus procedural safeguards." This answer allowed Taiwan's urban renewal to gradually recover from the standstill after 2012, and also made the review process for each urban renewal case longer and more expensive. The cost was shared by everyone, including the consenting owners who simply wanted to go home but waited more than four years.
