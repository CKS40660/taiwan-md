# Mona Rudao: The Anti-Japanese Hero Engraved on a Twenty Cent Coin, and the World Without China or Japan

> **30-Second Summary:** You might have had a twenty cent coin in your wallet, featuring the face of Seediq chief Mona Rudao. On October 27, 1930, he led six tribes in an uprising at Wushe Public School, killing over a hundred Japanese people before committing suicide by gun in a cave deep in the mountains; his remains were not found until three years later. Later, these remains served as anthropological specimens for nearly forty years and were reinterpreted by three different regimes as "savage evidence," "heroes of the Chinese nation resisting Japan," or "indigenous spirit of Taiwan." However, what he sought to defend was the _Seediq Bale_ (the Seediq language), which means "real people." The world he died defending had neither China nor Japan.

In July 2001, the Central Bank of Taiwan issued a new twenty cent coin. With a dual-tone design—gold on the outer ring and silver in the inner ring—it featured a dugout canoe from the Atayal tribe on the reverse, and a profile of Mona Rudao, the leader of the Wushe Incident, on the obverse. This was Taiwan's first circulating coin to feature an indigenous theme[^1].

However, this coin had an awkward origin. When the designers were engraving Mona’s face for the mold, they discovered that there was no photograph of him in domestic historical or cultural records. They eventually found one in a Japanese magazine and meticulously copied the portrait from it[^2].

In other words, the image of Taiwan's most famous anti-Japanese hero, the face recognized by the nation, was lifted from a Japanese publication.

What was even more awkward was that this coin circulated very little; so few people ever saw one in their lifetime. Some citizens tried to use it to buy snacks or drinks, but vendors refused it as counterfeit[^3]. A hero who was minted into currency and used to "promote ethnic harmony" had a coin that no one dared to accept.

This article seeks to examine the matter beneath these three layers of awkwardness: an anti-Japanese hero engraved on a twenty cent coin, whose true world of resistance contained neither China nor Japan.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> We are accustomed to placing Mona Rudao into a sentence structure: "He bravely resisted the Japanese as an indigenous leader." This statement is not wrong, but it presupposes a stage upon which "Japan" and "China" (or the "Chinese nation") stand. Mona chose to resist Japan. The problem is that this stage was constructed by later people. When Mona fired his weapons in 1930, the world he intended to defend was completely different from this stage. Only by understanding what he defended can we understand why this coin is so ironic.

## The Bridge He Wanted to Cross Was Called Rainbow

To understand Mona Rudao, one must first move beyond the framework of "anti-Japanese resistance" and enter the universe of the Seediq people.

Mona Rudao was a chief from the Mehebu community of the Tgdaya group of the Seediq, born around 1880 (according to Chinese and English Wikipedia), though some sources state 1882 (National Education Institute of Education). He left no household registration records, and even his birth year is disputed[^4]. In his youth, he was a fierce successor to the chief position, the wealthiest man in the tribe, and skilled at headhunting. Regarding his appearance, Wikipedia states that he "was tall and robust, reportedly nearly 190 cm," noting the word "reportedly." No one has ever measured his skeleton; this height is legend, not fact[^5].

In Mona's world, there was something called **Gaya**. It was a compilation of ancestral teachings, laws, social norms, and taboos, said to have been established by ancestral spirits (Utux) and unchangeable. Communal sacrifice, communal hunting, communal eating, adherence to taboos, and shared responsibility for sin were all collective obligations dictated by Gaya[^6].

One element within Gaya became the most misunderstood key aspect of the Wushe Incident: **headhunting** (_mgaya_ in Seediq). In the eyes of Han Chinese and the Japanese, headhunting was barbaric beheading. However, within the Seediq context, its meaning was far more complex than violence; it involved revenge, divine judgment (settling disputes based on the success or failure of a hunt), praying for a good harvest, mourning ancestors, and qualifying as an adult male[^7].

And all of this connected to a bridge. The Seediq believed that after death, one must cross a **Rainbow Bridge** (_Hakaw Utux_), beyond which resided the ancestral spirits. A crab-like spirit (Utux Kalan) guarded the bridge, checking your hands—or more precisely, checking your **tattooing**. Only men who had successfully hunted heads and women who knew how to weave cloth were qualified to have tattoos (_Ptasan_) on their faces. Those without tattoos could not cross the Rainbow Bridge and reach the ancestral spirits[^8].

Therefore, when the Japanese banned headhunting and tattooing, they severed far more than two customs. For the Seediq people, it meant severing the qualification to reunite with ancestors after death—it was the collapse of an entire system of identity and meaning. Taiwanese scholar Leo Ching (荊子馨) described the situation of Indigenous peoples under colonialism as a "colonial double-bind": being civilized into a "half-civilized person" while simultaneously being treated as savages by the colonizers, rejected by both sides[^9].

The Seediq language has a word, **Seediq Bale**, which means "true person" [^10]. What Mona Rudao sought to be was this: a person with facial tattoos who could cross the Rainbow Bridge after death and who upheld Gaya while alive. We later called him a hero, but the position of "hero" was assigned by others. All his life, he defended the status of a "true person."

Later, a film titled _Seediq Bale_ depicted Mona's world in grand scope. However, phrases like "sacrificing blood to ancestors" were actually invented by the director; there is no corresponding word in Seediq, something even the film's own cultural advisor for the Seediq later admitted[^11]. This reminds us of one thing: even the version we think is the "closest to the Seediq" might be told by others. Mona's world was difficult for outsiders to articulate from the start.

## The Unclean Hand

In Wushe during the 1930s, it was considered a "model subjugated territory" by the Japanese.

Japan's rule over Taiwan's indigenous peoples followed a path from leniency to severity. Initially managed by the Civil Affairs Department, it transitioned to police administration in 1906. From 1910 to 1915, the "Five-Year Pacification Plan" was implemented, led by Governor Sakuma Satomichi, which involved surveying approximately 16.3 million yen and utilizing military and police subjugation. Starting in 1915, indigenous peoples were disarmed and had their weapons confiscated, leading to a phase of assimilation where indigenous children's schools and public schools were established, Japanese was promoted, and practices such as tattooing, facial piercing, and tooth extraction were prohibited [^12].

By 1930, Wushe had been developed into a town comparable to a Japanese settlement. Even the revisionist historian Paul Barclay acknowledged that about 95% of the indigenous people there could communicate with Japanese police and teachers in simple Japanese [^13]. However, Barclay also pointed out that beneath this model lay an enclave dominated by outsiders, where the local people's quietness and loyalty were at best suspect [^14]. Beneath the surface of this model, resentment was accumulating.

There were several sources of resentment. First, forced labor. Between 1928 and 1930, indigenous peoples in the Wushe area were mobilized nine times for work. During the construction of a shrine in 1928, they were not only shortchanged on meal allowances but also forced to donate money. The wages were unfair: indigenous people received 20 to 30 cents per day, while Han Chinese workers received 60 cents [^15].

Second was the humiliation left by a political marriage. Japanese police officer Kondō Gijirō married Divas Rudao, Mona's sister. Later, Kondō was transferred to Hualien, and she disappeared (some say he was reassigned; others say he abandoned her). Divas was left behind, and according to the _Gaya_, a woman abandoned by her husband could not return to her family home [^16]. Barclay, in _Kondo the Barbarian_, verified that this "Kondō Gijirō" was the brother of another key subjugation official, Kondō Katsusaburō [^17]. A marriage arranged for the purpose of "subjugating indigenous peoples with indigenous peoples" ultimately became a thorn in the side of the Mona family.

The true spark, however, was a hand.

On October 7, 1930, Tado Mona (Tado Mona), the eldest son of Mona, offered a toast to Japanese police officer Yoshimura Katsumi at a wedding banquet. Tado had just slaughtered a pig and had animal blood on his hands. Yoshimura found them "unclean," refused the toast, and struck Tado's hand with a baton. A scuffle ensued between the two [^18]. Wikipedia describes it as follows:

> The eldest son of Mona, Tado Mona, attempted to toast Yoshimura, but Yoshimura refused, citing "disgust for the unclean feast...," and struck Tado Mona's hand with a baton, leading to a fight between the two parties [^19].

Afterward, Mona brought his tribe members to apologize to Yoshimura while carrying wine, but Yoshimura refused and threatened to report the incident. Under Japanese law, assaulting a police officer was a serious crime. With old grievances added to new hatreds, and fear of retribution, Mona made a decision: rather than wait for reckoning, he decided to revolt.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> There were three Japanese police officers in the Wushe Incident, each with a different role, and they deserve to be remembered separately: **Yoshimura Katsumi** was the one who struck Tado during the toast incident; **Kondō Gijirō** was the one who married Mona's sister and then abandoned her; later, **Kojima Genji** appears, who incited the Daoze group in the Second Wushe Incident. The Wushe Incident was never a story of one person against an empire; it was a web woven from many specific people and many concrete humiliations. And that "unclean hand" was merely the final straw that broke the camel's back.

## That Morning of the Sports Day

On October 27, 1930, Wushe Public School was hosting a joint sports day, gathering Japanese military police, schoolchildren, and family members at the stadium. Mo Na Lu Tao chose this day. In the early morning, about 300 men from six tribes attacked 13 police detachments around Wushe, seizing weapons and ammunition before rushing toward the sports field[^20]. The six tribes involved were Mahapo, Tarowan, Boalan, Sku, Hogo, and Rodao. Baran, which had the largest population and whose leader was Wallace Buny, did not participate. Key instigators were Bi-ho Sabu and Bi-ho Varis from the Hogo tribe[^21].

On that day, they killed 134 Japanese people, including women and children. They also mistakenly killed two Han Chinese individuals who wore kimono: Li Caiyun, a girl in a kimono, and Liu Cailiang, a shop owner hit by stray bullets[^22]. The tribes seized about 180 rifles and over 23,000 rounds of ammunition[^23].

The news reached Taipei, shaking the Governor's Office. The Japanese dispatched military forces and police, with Major General Kamata Michihiro in charge of the frontline (Governor Ishizuka Eizo was a civilian official, leaving the frontline to Major General Kamata). Bombing and mountain artillery attacks were used, and the suppression operation continued for about fifty days until early December[^24].

The most difficult and controversial page during the suppression was that of poison gas. Many Chinese sources state that the Japanese military used vesicant (Lewisite or mustard) gas, and even white phosphorus bombs. Testimonies from the tribes mentioned "skin blistering." The Taiwan People's Party also telegraphed the International League in early 1931 to protest the Japanese use of "poison gas massacre"[^25]. However, academia has no definitive conclusion on this matter. Japanese historian Akimichi Haruzan argued that the Japanese military actually used "several hundred tear gas bombs, plus at least three special gases (containing cyanide and tear gas components)," rather than mustard gas. The Japanese Wikipedia even states, "It is not yet clear"[^26].

Therefore, this article will not assert that "the Japanese army massacred with poison gas," as that exceeds the scope of evidence. However, one document proves at least one thing: the Japanese knew they were doing something clandestine. On November 5, 1930, a secret telegram from an assistant to the War Ministry was sent to the Taiwan Military Chief of Staff, stating that the use of vesicant munitions would not be discussed due to foreign relations concerns, and that all future matters involving gas bombs must be communicated using code[^27]. This telegram is stored in archives at the Japanese Ministry of Defense (JACAR, S5-2-26). It demanded not a cessation of poison gas use, but that it not be put on paper—this was concrete evidence of deliberate concealment[^28]. Because of this protest, the Taiwan People's Party was forcibly dissolved in February 1931. Japan did not sign the Geneva Protocol banning chemical weapons until 1975[^29].

During the suppression, an event occurred that has been repeatedly written about and romanticized: the mass suicide of women and children. The number mentioned varies; Chinese sources state approximately 296 people, while English Wikipedia states around 290[^30]. They hanged themselves under a large tree so as not to burden the men who were still fighting, allowing the soldiers peace of mind to continue their battle. Movies portray this scene as heroic sacrifice. However, descendants from the Qingtian tribe understand it as "no choice" desperation: starvation and cold in the cave, where the way to live had already been blocked[^31].

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> The difference between these two readings is ultimately a matter of "who has the right to give meaning to the dead." "Heroic sacrifice" is a word chosen by those in the audience; "no choice" is how descendants understood it within the context of the cave. This article chooses to stand on the latter side, and chooses not to reconstruct the details of that morning one by one—not writing methods, nor exaggerating appearances, but merely recording the time, place, and what a tribe did when driven to desperation. This was an event from Gaya, not a spectacle.

As for Mo Na Lu Tao himself, his date of death is a structural mystery. Because his remains were only found in 1933, the date of death is essentially estimated; various versions range from November 5, mid-November, November 28, to December 1. This article adopts "around late November 1930" and notes that this date is uncertain[^32]. What is certain is that he committed suicide with a Type 38 rifle in a cave on the right bank of Mahapo Stream.

One scene in the movie shows Mo Na shooting his wife to death. But this is an adaptation. A 1936 Japanese publication claimed that Mo Na killed his wife and children, but tribal members who experienced this event later confirmed that Mo Na's wife actually committed suicide by hanging because **killing her and the children violated the Gaya of the Sediq tribe**[^33]. Wikipedia recorded this correction:

> Because killing her and the children violates the Gaya of the Sediq tribe, it was confirmed by tribal members who experienced the event that Mo Na Lu Tao's wife committed suicide by hanging[^34].

Regarding the number of dead, different sources contradict each other. Here, we do not settle on one figure but lay out the discrepancies: Chinese Wikipedia breaks down the deaths of the six tribes into 85 from weapons, 137 from bombing, 34 from artillery shells, 87 from beheading, and 296 from suicide, totaling 639 people, plus 265 prisoners and about 500 who surrendered, making a total of 1,236 participants. English Wikipedia records approximately 1,200 participants, 644 dead, and 290 suicides[^35]. The numbers do not match, but the direction is consistent: the six tribes involved numbered over a thousand, with nearly half dying in this uprising.

## The Half Not Taught in Textbooks

If the story ended here, it would be a clean tragedy: an indigenous group uniting to resist tyranny and making a heroic sacrifice. But the true second half of the Wushe Incident is barely taught in textbooks because it is far from clean. On April 25, 1931, the "Second Wushe Incident" occurred. After the survivors of the Six Tribes surrendered, they were detained at the "Protective Reservation" in Seibō and Rodao. Japanese police officer Kojima Genji (the one mentioned earlier) incited pro-Japanese members of the Daoze group to form a "Mikata Ban Attack Squad" of about 200 people, who attacked the reservation by night. A total of 216 people were killed or committed suicide (different sources cite 214, 216, or 218), and 101 heads were severed—a number supported by a photograph in the National Taiwan Museum of History Collection (accession number 2017.025.0192.0019) [^36]. The Japanese side even issued bounties: 200 yen for a leader's head, 100 yen for an able-bodied man, 30 yen for a woman, and 20 yen for a child. Afterward, the Daoze group was given the lands of the Six Tribes [^37].

```tw-stat

200 / 100 / 30 / 20 yen | Bounties for leader/able-bodied man/woman/child | Issued by the Japanese side
Source: National Taiwan Museum of History Collection 2017.025.0192.0019, Chinese Wikipedia, China Times

In other words, the last group to kill the Wushe insurgents were other indigenous people. Indigenous people killed indigenous people.

The Japanese police deliberately exploited old hunting grounds feuds between various indigenous groups to have them attack surviving members of the same tribe during the Second Wushe Incident](/article-images/people/musha-mikata-ban-1931.webp)
_The "Mikata Ban" (pro-Japanese indigenous forces) mobilized by the Japanese side (1931). Photo: Kohei Ebidara's "Wushe Campaign Photographs." [Public domain via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mikata-Ban2.JPG)._

The leader of this force was **Temuj Varis** (circa 1898 to November 11, 1930), from the Daoze group. He was almost a mirror image of Mona. The Daoze and Degudaya groups had an ancient feud over hunting grounds, which the Japanese deliberately exacerbated; Kojima Genji exploited this old grievance [^38]. Mona's wife once tried to recruit Temuj to join the uprising, but he refused, instead hiding Kojima. Finally, Temuj led the attack force in pursuit of the insurgents, where they were ambushed and killed by the Degudaya group in Habon Valley, with over a dozen subordinates dying in battle [^39].

The movie portrays Mona and Temuj as mortal enemies, but that is fiction. The Seediq translator Iwan Pering points out that according to _Gaya_, Mona could not have invaded another tribe's hunting grounds; the two might even have been related by marriage [^40]. Temuj was not a villain; he was a leader following the same logic of _Gaya_ but standing on the opposing side. To demonize him as a "pro-Japanese traitor" is an act of convenience by later generations. Guo Ming-cheng (Dakis Pawan), a cultural consultant for the Seediq, rejects this view. He states that when promising someone something, the Seediq must see it through; this is the spirit of _Seediq Bale_ [^41].

Between the extremes of Mona and Temuj, there was a third type of person: **Hanaoka Ichiro** and **Hanaoka Jiro**.

These two men were not related; both came from the Hogo tribe and were "modelized" by Japan as elites who received Japanese education and became Seediq police. Scholar Nakao Eki Pacidal calls them the "inbetweeners" [^42]. When the incident broke out, they made the most painful choice. Hanaoka Ichiro (Dakis Nobing of the Seediq) had studied at Taichung Normal School; he first took care of his family and then stabbed himself in the abdomen with a _fan_ knife (not Japanese samurai-style seppuku). Hanaoka Jiro (Dakis Nawi) led his people to commit ritual suicide [^43]. They left a will written in Japanese, which began:

> 我等は此の世を去らねばならぬ／蕃人のこうふんは出役の多い為にこんな事件になりました (We must leave this world / The rebellion of the indigenous people was caused by too much labor service) [^44].

Hanaoka Jiro's wife, Takayama Hatsuko (Dakis Tadau of the Seediq), survived and later became a key witness to the Wushe Incident in the research of Deng Xiangyang [^45]. Her son, Hanaoka Koga (Awi Dakis, 1930–2001), later became the head of Ren'ai Township, and he was the one who recovered Mona's remains in 1973 [^46]. The Japanese did not trust them, nor did the Seediq necessarily accept them. The Hanaoka brothers died caught between two worlds; even their final words to posterity were written in Japanese.

After the incident concluded, the survivors paid the ultimate price. On May 6, 1931, approximately 298 people from the surviving Six Tribes (Japanese documents mention 278) were forcibly relocated to "Chuanzhong Island." Although the name contains the character for island, it is actually a plateau surrounded by the Beigang Creek, Meiyuan Creek, and Abis Creek. They were forbidden from returning home; four people attempted to go back, and three were executed. Dysentery and malaria spread in the detention area [^47]. The Japanese authorities also held individuals accountable: Governor-General Ishizuka Eizo and Director-General Hanjuro Mitsuhiro resigned on January 16, 1931, and Police Bureau Chief Ishii Yasushi and Taichung Prefectural Governor Mizukoshi Kōichi were dismissed [^48].

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> Why do textbooks only teach the first half and not the second? Because the first half fits into the clean framework of "the Chinese nation resisting Japan," but the second half does not. The Second Wushe Incident, the Daoze group, indigenous people killing indigenous people—when these facts are laid bare, the myth of "a single nation uniting to resist tyranny" is shattered. Revisionist historian Barclay exposed this cleanliness: the Daoze and Taroko groups had cooperated with Japan to hunt down insurgents; the fuse for the Wushe Incident was immediate humiliation and forced labor, not a systematic plan of extermination [^49]. However, Barclay also emphasized that he was not whitewashing colonialism; the Japanese response to the insurgents was indeed one of "genocidal fury." What he shattered was the clean cloak of nationalism draped over this uprising, not the legitimacy of the uprising itself.

## A Specimen That Traveled for Forty Years

Mona Rudao is dead, but his story has not ended. Because of his remains, he was about to embark on a strange journey lasting nearly forty years. On July 6, 1933, hunters from the Poalu tribe discovered a set of remains in a rock cave on the right bank of the Mahepo Stream. Mona's daughter, Mahong Mona, identified them as her father by his clothing, silver bracelets, and _banto_ (indigenous knife)[^50].

![A photo of the Wushe Incident site from the Japanese "Wushe Subjugation Photo Album"; mountains and settlements are visible in the distance. The Japanese compiled the entire conquest into a photo album for public printing and exhibition](/article-images/people/musha-incident-scene-1930.webp)

What followed remains painful to read today. On June 13, 1934, upon the completion of the Nanto District Office, Mona's bones were put on public display as an exhibit, attracting nearly ten thousand spectators. On July 1 of the same year, they were placed in a glass display case at a police exhibition in the botanical garden. It is recorded that the exhibit that most interested the audience was none other than Mona's bones[^51]. On the 28th of the same month, the remains were sent to Taihoku Imperial University and received by Ukawa Kojiro of the Department of Ethnology; they were later subjected to so-called "big foot" research by anatomist Kanagari Takeo. From then on, Mona Rudao became a numbered anthropological specimen, left untouched for approximately forty years[^52].

Source: Historical materials and research regarding the Wushe Incident and the discovery, exhibition, and reburial of Mona Rudao's remains[^50][^51][^52]
```

The person who rescued Mona from the specimen cabinet was an anthropologist. On September 17, 1973, Li Yi-yuan, acting chair of the Department of Archaeology and Anthropology at National Taiwan University (NTU), wrote to President Yen Chen-hsing suggesting the return of the remains. On December 24 of the same year, Mona Rudao left NTU[^53].

There is a bitter cycle here: anthropology was what turned him into a specimen "for research," and anthropology was also what finally released him from the specimen cabinet. A man who rose up specifically so as not to be treated as "evidence of a primitive people" spent forty years after death serving exactly that purpose—and that was precisely what the colonizers used to "prove" during the Wushe Incident: how "primitive" and "savage" the Indigenous peoples were[^54].

Even his return home was not done on his own terms. The reburial ceremony was presided over by Yeh Tung-min, Chairman of the Taiwan Provincial Government, using a complete set of Han Chinese rituals: a funeral hall, wreaths, mourning music, and a hearse; even a Chinese-style white memorial archway was erected before the grave, inscribed with "Heroic Blood and Gallant Spirit" (碧血英風) and "Loyal Heart and Courageous Soul" (義膽忠肝)[^55]. Even returning home, he did not walk across his own Rainbow Bridge.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> From 1934 to 1973, the same remains were defined by three different powers in turn. The Japanese treated him as "evidence of savagery" and made him a specimen for exhibition; the Kuomintang (KMT) government treated him as a "Chinese hero resisting Japan," erecting monuments, enshrining him in the Martyrs' Shrine, and issuing commendations; after localization, he became a symbol of "Taiwanese local consciousness." Michael Berry, editor of _The Musha Incident: A Reader_ (Columbia University Press, 2022), explains this process clearly: three regimes, three sets of usages, each of which pushed the Seediq people out of the position of subjects in the narrative. Mona being engraved on a twenty-dollar coin is actually a third appropriation.

Berry summarizes these three usages in the book as follows (translated from English):

> The Japanese used it to prove the "savagery" of Indigenous peoples; the KMT government cited this uprising as evidence of Taiwanese bravery and their solidarity with China in resisting Japan; while pro-independence groups use it as an example of "authentic" cultural tradition[^56].

This line of appropriation extends to the present day. In 1969, Mona Rudao was enshrined in the Martyrs' Shrine, recorded as the first Indigenous person to be so honored. In 1970, the Executive Yuan issued a commendation signed by Minister of the Interior Hsu Ching-chung[^57]. The Wushe memorial monument has been altered time and again: in 1950, Gao Yung-ching first demolished the shrine and replaced it with the "Remembrance Monument of a Life Lived" (餘生紀念碑). In 1953, the "Heroic Blood and Gallant Spirit" archway and the Martyrdom Memorial were erected, signed by Taiwan Provincial Chairman Wu Kuo-chen. After Wu Kuo-chen became embroiled in a political scandal, the inscription was changed to the "Wushe Mountain People Uprising Memorial" (霧社山胞抗日起義紀念碑) and signed by Huang Chieh[^58]. Historian Ku Heng-chan noted in his article "Flowing Memories" that every time the regime changes, the signatory changes and the inscription is re-carved; this is how the state carves political memory into the same piece of stone[^59].

As for that twenty-dollar coin, the official reason given by the Central Bank is "to respect the history and culture of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples and promote ethnic harmony"[^60]. It sounds very good. But when you know that his face was sourced from a Japanese magazine, that no one dares to accept that coin, and that those remains served as a specimen for forty years, those six words—"promote ethnic harmony"—take on a different kind of weight.

## Whose Hero

In 2011, the film _Seediq Bale_, directed by Wei De-sheng (魏德聖), was released in two parts: _The Sun Flag_ and _Rainbow Bridge_. The combined box office revenue in Taiwan reached approximately 880 million NTD. It was nominated for the main competition at the Venice Film Festival, won Best Picture at the Golden Horse Awards, and was among the nine finalists for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film[^61]. For Taiwanese people born after 1985, the face of Mona Rudao, as portrayed by the Taíya (泰雅) pastor Lin Ching-te (林慶台), became the image of adult Mona.

The film is responsible for keeping Mona in memory across an entire generation. However, it is also the version that scholars most critically question as "romanticized."

The harshest critic was Dakis Pawan (郭明正), a descendant of the Qīngliú tribe and author of _Truth: Bale_ (《真相·巴萊\*). He pointed out that terms like "ancestral blood sacrifice" and "pride" were invented by Wei De-sheng, as they do not exist in the Seediq language; he noted that Mona shooting his wife violated _Gaya_ (a cultural concept), which he warned Wei De-sheng about, but who proceeded with filming; and that the rebellion was romanticized into a personal male achievement[^62]. The Indigenous writer Walis Pawan criticized the film's individualism as violating the tradition of collective decision-making: traditional tribal leadership follows collective decisions, and no single person acts unilaterally[^63]. Scholar Lin Chin-ru (林津如) systematically organized these cultural distortions in her paper on CLCWeb (2018)[^64].

If _Seediq Bale_ was a spectacle, then the 2014 documentary _Afterlife—Seediq Bale_ (《餘生—賽德克·巴萊》), by Tang Xiang-zhu (湯湘竹), is its counterpart. This film was nominated for Best Documentary and Best Sound at the 50th Golden Horse Awards (though it did not win). It follows the descendants of the survivors to seek the origin of ancestral spirits, Pusu Qhuni (牡丹岩), responding from the perspective of those who lived through it, in contrast to the grandeur on screen[^65]. Everyone writes about the climax of the resistance, but few write about "how to live after surviving." The novelist Wu He’s _Afterlife_ also follows this path; he interviews descendants and criticizes Lee Teng-hui's monument and Chen Shui-bian's coins for politicizing Mona rather than truly mourning him; the survivors actually lived in a state of shameful silence[^66].

The modern mosaic continues. On April 23, 2008, the Seediq people separated from the Taíya tribe and were officially recognized as Taiwan's 14th Indigenous group, divided into three dialect groups: Degudataya, Daozhe, and Deruqu[^67]. On Indigenous Peoples' Day on August 1, 2016, President Tsai Ing-wen (蔡英文) apologized to the Indigenous peoples on behalf of the government:

> For four hundred years, every regime that has come to Taiwan has severely infringed upon the existing rights of the Indigenous peoples through military conquest and land plunder[^68].

She also established the Committee for Historical Justice and Transitional Justice of Indigenous Peoples, with Dr. Cai Chi-wei (蔡志偉), a Seediq doctoral candidate in law (Awi Mona, Taiwan's first Indigenous legal scholar), serving as the convener of the land subcommittee[^69]. But justice was not achieved here. The 2017 regulations on traditional territory excluded private land, reducing the area available for demarcation from 1.8 million hectares to about 800,000 hectares, sparking protests that lasted several years in Kaohsiung[^70].

The sharpest question is left to the contemporary era. In 2025, _Seediq Bale_ was re-released in China under the name "80th Anniversary of Restoration." A Seediq descendant, Walis Pawan, responded during an interview (translated from a English report):

> This is a matter between China and Japan. They use a film about us to speak for our feelings, but China has never come to ask us[^71].

This sentence can be considered the key to the entire article. Mona Rudao was claimed by three parties: Han Chinese/Taiwanese nationalism, the narrative of the Chinese nation, and the silence of the descendants of the Wais (味方蕃). These three parties held irreconcilable relationships with the same person. And that person who was repeatedly appropriated was never asked[^72].

## The Real Person

Returning to the Gluban village.

Descendants of those forcibly relocated to Chuanzhong Island back then still live here, in a place called Gluban, which administratively belongs to Ren'ai Township, Nantou County. A type of rice called "Chuanzhong Rice" (Taiwan Rice No. 9) is grown here; during the Japanese colonial period, it was sent as tribute to the Emperor. Media often refers to this as an "indigenous village with the highest level of education and the densest concentration of civil servants," although this description lacks official statistical support and can only be considered a common narrative[^73]. The village has a memorial and museum dedicated to Yu Sheng. The golden rice fields are harvested year after year. At the end of _Yu Sheng_ by Tang Xiangzhu, descendants are still searching in the mountains for the ancestral origin point called Pusu Qhuni[^74].

![A statue of Mona Rudao from Wushe, Ren'ai Township, Nantou County, with the Wushe Uprising Monument in the background. This is the image created by the state, separated from the "real person" he wished to be, by an entire set of rewritten memory politics](/article-images/people/mona-rudao-statue-monument-2012.webp)
_The statue and uprising monument of Mona Rudao in Wushe. Photo: Xu Fanglan (fanglan), 2012. [CC BY 2.0 via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mona_Rudao_Statue_and_Wushe_Incident_Monument,taken_by_fanglan.jpg)._

We need heroes. We need a clean story that can fit into textbooks, we need a coin that can be held in the palm of our hand, and we need a face to hang on the wall. So, we minted Mona Rudao onto a twenty-cent piece, wrote him into "the Chinese nation's resistance against Japan," and filmed him as a heroic epic.

But Mona Rudao never wanted to be a hero. What he wanted to be was a Seediq Bale—a real person. Someone with facial tattoos, who guarded _Gaya_, and who crossed the Rainbow Bridge after death. In that world, there was no China, no Japan; only ancestral spirits, the hunting grounds, that bridge, and the crab spirit checking your hands at the bridgehead.

The face on the coin was taken from a Japanese magazine. The remains were specimens for nearly forty years before being buried with Han rituals, never crossing his own Rainbow Bridge to go home. We gave him everything except one thing—to treat him as a person, not a monument.

Perhaps the first step in truly knowing Mona Rudao is not remembering how many Japanese he killed, but understanding the world he died defending, a world that did not need us to choose sides for it.
