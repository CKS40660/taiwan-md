> **30-Second Overview:**
> Wang Da-hong (1917–2018) is hailed as the "Father of Modern Architecture in Taiwan" and an "Architectural Poet" [^1] [^2]. As the son of Wang Chung-hui (王寵惠), a former Minister of Foreign Affairs for the Republic of China [^3], he studied engineering at Cambridge University and architecture at Harvard University, studying under Bauhaus founder Walter Gropius. Returning to Taiwan with cutting-edge Western modernist training, he chose to spend his life grappling with the "conflict between tradition and modernity." From the controversial and heavily revised Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall to his lost and subsequently reconstructed residence on Jianguo South Road, Wang used minimalist, calm geometric lines to embed a literati-style Eastern solitude between concrete and glass.

In September 1965, Taipei's architectural community witnessed an unprecedented competition: the government sought designs for a memorial hall to honor Dr. Sun Yat-sen by inviting twelve renowned architects to submit proposals [^4] [^5]. In an era desperate to prove legitimacy and grandeur both domestically and internationally, most designs featured massive palace-style roofs, dense eaves, and dazzling yellow glazed tiles. However, the winning entry was unexpectedly quiet—a low-slung, horizontally extended modernist building with a subtly and exquisitely upturned roof, designed by the then 48-year-old Wang Da-hong [^4] [^6].

This victory did not bring immediate applause or smooth sailing; instead, it marked the beginning of decades of solitude, as his aesthetic remained out of step with the prevailing zeitgeist.

## A Bauhaus Successor from Cambridge and Harvard

Born in Beijing on July 6, 1917, Wang's father, Wang Chung-hui, had served as the Minister of Foreign Affairs and President of the Judicial Yuan for the Republic of China [^3] [^7]. His unique family background allowed him to travel through Europe during his youth, witnessing the rise of Western modern art and architectural movements. He first entered the University of Cambridge to major in mechanical engineering before crossing the ocean to the United States to study architecture at the Harvard Graduate School of Design (GSD), where he was a contemporary of I. M. Pei [^7].

At Harvard, his mentor was none other than Walter Gropius, the founder of the Bauhaus—the cradle of the modernist design movement. Gropius taught his students that architecture was not a display of power or a collage of historical styles, but an honest response to contemporary materials, structures, and the real needs of human life. This training was etched into Wang's very core: his drawings always featured minimalist lines, precise proportions, pure white walls, and expansive glass surfaces.

However, when he returned to post-war Taiwan carrying these pure modernist ideals, he was met by a society eager to find national symbols and political legitimacy through visual cues. The "Less is More" philosophy and unadorned rationality of modernism appeared too avant-garde on the streets of Taipei at the time, and some decision-makers even viewed it as "not Chinese enough."

## Compromise and Persistence at the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall: When Modernism Met the Traditional Palace

In Wang Da-hong's career, the design of the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was undoubtedly his greatest and most agonizing creative ordeal [^8].

"The Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was my most difficult design... The Father of the Nation was a revolutionary, so the architecture commemorating him must be innovative; yet his character and lifestyle were characterized by simplicity and modesty, so the building should not lapse into luxury or vulgarity," Wang remarked in an interview during his later years [^5]. Initially, he submitted a purely modernist proposal that stripped away all traditional symbols in favor of a contemporary sculptural feel, but it was rejected by high-level officials.

To ensure the building could be completed, he integrated traditionally symbolic upturned eaves into the framework of the modern structure [^6] [^8]. This was not a craftsman-like imitation, but a modernized abstraction achieved through rigorous geometric calculation: the eaves lifted slightly, evoking traditional imagery without the heaviness and complexity of palace architecture. Although the final completion sparked much controversy regarding this eclectic solution—with some critics arguing the upturned eaves were inconsistent with tradition—architectural scholars looking back years later realized it was a rare example in public architecture where personal will was successfully fused with modern structure [^6] [^8] [^9].

### A Dialogue Between Modernism and Local Tradition

In Wang's design philosophy, "tradition" was never a set of symbols to be copied or replicated, but rather a sense of spatial soul and proportion.

📝 **Curator's Note:**
The tragedy and greatness of Wang Da-hong lie in his constant attempt to insist on "the honesty and dignity of space itself" during an era that "demanded political correctness and symbolic representation." He used the blade of modernism to carve through the traditional Chinese robe; the pain was inevitable, but it left behind an irreplaceable slice of history.

## The Jianguo South Road Residence: An Architect's Ultimate Honesty

In contrast to the compromises made in his public works, Wang Da-hong demonstrated his purest, most unadulterated architectural aesthetic in his private residences. In 1953, he personally designed and built his own home on Jianguo South Road in Taipei [^10].

Though the single-story house had a modest footprint, it has become a legend in the history of modern residential architecture in Taiwan. It features white exterior walls, black tiles, circular moon gates, a small courtyard with an Eastern garden atmosphere, and minimalist furniture tailored entirely to internal functional needs [^10] [^11]. Wang did not treat it as a mansion to flaunt his status, but as a modern "Peach Blossom Spring" (a utopia) where a literati soul could find peace.

However, due to the rapid urban renewal of Taipei, this residence faced demolition in the 2010s. Fortunately, through the efforts of the Wang Da-hong Architecture Research and Preservation Association and various experts, the Taipei Fine Arts Museum reconstructed the building in 2017 within the Art Park adjacent to the museum [^10] [^11].

Walking into the reconstructed Jianguo South Road residence, one is struck by an extreme sense of stillness. Sunlight filters through the moon gates onto the terrazzo floors; every corner of the interior and the proportion of every table and chair reveals the owner's strict discipline toward life. It is not just a house, but a sonnet written in concrete, light, and shadow.

## Unfinished Ambitions and the Literary World: From Moon Landing Monuments to _Phantasmagoria_

Beyond architectural design, Wang's talents were not limited to steel and concrete. He participated in an international design competition following the 1969 Apollo moon landing, designing a forward-looking "Moon Landing Monument" that showcased his poetic imagination regarding human technology and the macrocosm of the universe.

In his later years, he turned to writing English science fiction, composing _Phantasmagoria_ (_Huan Cheng_). With delicate and fluent English prose, he constructed a utopian world merging Eastern philosophy with future technology. This book reflects not only his profound erudition but also the childlike wonder and curiosity toward the unknown that he maintained deep within.

## The Solitude of an Architectural Poet and Its Echoes Through Time

On May 28, 2018, Wang Da-hong passed away peacefully at the age of 101 [^2]. Throughout his long life, he designed numerous influential works, including the Ministry of Foreign Affairs building, National Taiwan University dormitories, Hong Lu (虹廬), and the TCC Building [^12] [^13]. He was always seen in a sharp suit, speaking with a gentle yet firm manner, like one of the last true gentlemen from the early Republican era.

For a long time, Taiwanese society did not fully understand him. The public chased larger commercial towers and more flamboyant decorations, while he simply sat quietly in the chairs he designed, smoking, watching the light and shadow move across white walls.

It was only in his later years and after his passing, as Taiwan's urban landscape began to appear cluttered due to excessive ornamentation, that people suddenly looked back. They realized this architectural poet had provided the answer half a century ago: architecture does not need to shout; it only needs to be honest, quiet, and leave room for emptiness.
